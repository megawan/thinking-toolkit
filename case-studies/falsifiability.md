# Case study: The 1919 eclipse test of general relativity

> Documented example. It is based on the source listed below, and the details are limited to what that source reports.

Method: [Falsifiability](../methods/critical-thinking/falsifiability.md)

## Context

Einstein's general relativity made a specific, checkable prediction: light from a star passing close to the Sun should be bent by gravity. The theory predicted a deflection of about 1.75 arcseconds at the Sun's edge, which is twice the value of a Newtonian-style calculation, about 0.83 arcseconds. (Einstein's incomplete 1911 paper had given the lower figure; he corrected it in 1915.) Because stars near the Sun are normally invisible, the test needed a total solar eclipse.

## What was done

The Astronomer Royal, Frank Dyson, and Arthur Eddington organised expeditions, run by a joint committee of the Royal Society and the Royal Astronomical Society, to observe the total eclipse of 29 May 1919. They chose that eclipse because the Sun would stand in front of the bright Hyades star group. Two teams observed from:

- Príncipe, an island off West Africa (Eddington and Edwin Cottingham)
- Sobral, in Ceará, Brazil (Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson)

Photographs of the stars were compared with photographs of the same field taken when the Sun was elsewhere, to see whether the star positions had shifted.

## Outcome

- At Príncipe, clouds degraded the plates
- At Sobral, the main telescope gave blurred images, which were discarded (their deflections were closer to the Newtonian prediction); a smaller 4-inch telescope gave the clearest images and a deflection slightly above the Einsteinian prediction
- A historian cited on the page, Daniel Kennefick, holds that without the Sobral photographs the result would have been inconclusive
- The results were presented at a joint meeting of the Royal Society and the Royal Astronomical Society on 6 November 1919 and published in 1920, and the press coverage began Einstein's international fame
- Astronomers argued for about a decade over the exact values, including how to weight different stars and possible systematic effects; only radio-astronomy measurements in the late 1960s definitively showed the full general-relativity value

## Lessons

- The prediction was specific and distinguishable from the alternative: the two numbers were about a factor of two apart, so the test could have gone against the theory
- The criteria were set by the theories before the photographs were taken, which is what makes a test fair
- Real tests are messy: weather, blurred images and choices in analysis all mattered, and the argument over data analysis lasted years
- Repeated, independent confirmation (later eclipses and radio measurements) mattered more than any single result

## Caveats

- The source is a Wikipedia article, only part of which was read verbatim; the remainder rests on a summary and is not used here
- Karl Popper is widely reported to have used this test as an example of a risky prediction, but the pages read for this entry do not confirm it; the Falsifiability article, in the part read, discusses risky predictions in general and does not mention the eclipse
- The page does not give the numerical results of the 1919 measurements, so none are quoted
- Philosophers criticise strict falsificationism (the Duhem-Quine thesis, Lakatos, Kuhn), and this case also shows how a test depends on background assumptions
