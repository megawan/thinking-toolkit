# Case study: Hormone therapy and the Women's Health Initiative

> Documented example. It is based on the source listed below, and the details are limited to what that source reports. It describes a research finding and is not medical advice.

Method: [Correlation vs Causation](../methods/critical-thinking/correlation-vs-causation.md)

## Context

The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) is a set of studies started by the United States National Institutes of Health in 1991. One part tested hormone therapy (HT) for postmenopausal women in randomised, placebo-controlled trials. The hypothesis was that HT would reduce coronary heart disease (CHD) and fractures. According to the source, when the trial was designed no clinical trial had tested HT's effect on the heart, and the belief that it might help rested on observational studies alone.

## What was done

- Two regimens were each compared with placebo: estrogen alone for women who had had a hysterectomy, and estrogen plus progestin for women with a uterus
- Enrolment was 16,608 women in the estrogen-plus-progestin trial and 10,739 in the estrogen-alone trial
- Participants were postmenopausal women aged 50 to 79, with an average age of 63
- Because women were assigned by chance, the groups should have been alike apart from the treatment
- The primary outcome was CHD; the primary adverse outcome was breast cancer

## Outcome

- **Estrogen plus progestin:** interim monitoring showed increased risks of breast cancer, CHD, stroke and pulmonary embolism, which outweighed the benefits seen for colorectal cancer and fractures. The pills were stopped in July 2002 after an average follow-up of 5.2 years. For CHD the page's table gives a hazard ratio of 1.24 (95% interval 1.00 to 1.54).
- **Estrogen alone:** stopped in February 2004 after an average follow-up of 6.8 years, with no apparent effect on heart disease (hazard ratio 0.95, interval 0.79 to 1.15) and an increased risk of stroke
- The authors recommended that hormone therapy not be prescribed to prevent chronic disease in postmenopausal women
- The page notes that the estrogen-alone result was in contrast to the findings of earlier observational studies

## Lessons

- An association seen in observational data did not hold up when chance decided who got the treatment
- Observational comparisons can be misleading when the people who choose a treatment differ from those who do not; random assignment removes that difference
- A well-designed trial can contradict a widely accepted belief
- The question of *who* was studied still matters: the page notes that the average participant was 63, so women near menopause were under-represented

## Caveats

- The source is a Wikipedia article; only the first part was read verbatim, and the rest rests on a summary that is not used here
- How the gap between the observational and trial findings should be explained (for example selection effects or the age at which therapy starts) is discussed in parts of the page that were not read verbatim, and no explanation is asserted here
- The page gives no separate start year for the hormone trials, only the 1991 announcement of the WHI
- Later analyses refined these findings; this entry reports only what the source states and is not guidance for any patient
