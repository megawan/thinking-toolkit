# Case study: Russell's teapot

> Documented example. It is based on the source listed below, and the details are limited to what that source reports. It is a case about an argument and how it was used, not about a decision.

Method: [Burden of Proof](../methods/critical-thinking/burden-of-proof.md)

## Context

In 1952 the philosopher Bertrand Russell wrote an article titled "Is There a God?", which was not published until after his death. In it he opened by noting that many orthodox people behave as if sceptics must disprove received dogmas, when the dogmatists should prove them, and he called that a mistake. To illustrate the point he used an analogy that became known as Russell's teapot.

## What was done

Russell asked readers to suppose he claimed that a china teapot orbits the Sun in an elliptical orbit between Earth and Mars, too small to be seen by even our most powerful telescopes. Nobody could disprove the claim. But, he said, it would be nonsense to argue that because the claim cannot be disproved, doubting it is intolerable presumption on the part of human reason.

He then added a twist: suppose the teapot's existence were affirmed in ancient books, taught as sacred truth every Sunday and instilled in children at school. Then doubting it would be treated as eccentric, and in an enlightened age the doubter would be sent to a psychiatrist (in an earlier age, to the Inquisitor). Russell applied the analogy to religion.

## Outcome

The analogy became one of the best-known illustrations of where the burden of proof lies: with the person who makes an empirically unfalsifiable claim, not with everyone else. Later writers used or echoed it:

- Richard Dawkins used it against what he called "agnostic conciliation" in *A Devil's Chaplain* (2003) and *The God Delusion* (2006)
- Similar earlier and later analogies include J. B. Bury's race of English-speaking donkeys on a planet around Sirius (1913) and Carl Sagan's invisible, floating "Dragon in the Garage" (1995)

## Lessons

- A claim that cannot be tested cannot be disproved, and that does not make it likely
- The claimant, not the doubter, is expected to bring the evidence
- The more a claim is surrounded by authority and social pressure, the harder it is to ask for evidence, so the principle matters most there
- The argument concerns *who must supply evidence*; it does not say how much evidence is enough in a given setting

## Caveats

- The source is a Wikipedia article; Russell's original article was not read for this entry
- The analogy is disputed. Critics cited on the page include Brian Garvey (it fails for religion, because theists and atheists disagree about whole explanations of the world, not about one item), Peter van Inwagen (the logical form is unclear), Alvin Plantinga (there is plenty of evidence against the teapot) and Paul Chamberlain (every truth claim, positive or negative, has a burden of proof)
- The page's view that the teapot is a good example is shared by some and rejected by others; this entry uses it only for what it shows about the burden of proof, not for its conclusion about religion
