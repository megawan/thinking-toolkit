# Case study: The Hanoi rat hunt, 1902

> Documented example. It is based on the sources listed below, and the details are limited to what those sources report.

Method: [Second-order Thinking](../methods/mental-models/second-order-thinking.md)

## Context

In the early 1900s, the French colonial government in Hanoi, under Governor-General Paul Doumer, was building a modern city with wide avenues, villas and a new sewer system. According to an account of a talk by the historian Michael Vann, the sewers gave plague-carrying rats a convenient home, and plague hit the French neighbourhood hard. The authorities were alarmed.

## What was done

The government first hired Vietnamese rat-catching squads. The account reports that thousands of rats were killed in the first week and that 20,114 were culled on the peak day, 12 June, but that population modelling showed the squads could not keep pace with breeding.

The French then offered a bounty of one cent to anyone in the city who brought in a rat tail. Officials chose the tail as proof because they did not want to handle thousands of carcasses.

## Outcome

The first-order effect was as intended: tails were handed in at unprecedented rates within weeks. The second-order effects were not:

- Tail-less rats were seen hobbling around the city, because people cut off the tails and let the rats live and keep breeding
- Farmers on the outskirts began breeding rats to collect the bounty
- The French were furious at being outsmarted, and that the money they spent was adding to the rat problem
- Vann says the authorities then turned to harsher methods, which led to protests by residents. The account gives no clear end date for the bounty or final figures.

## Lessons

- The bounty rewarded handing in tails, not reducing the rat population, so the incentive and the goal came apart
- The people who responded were the ones with the most to gain: residents, who could earn money by breeding or releasing rats
- Asking "what behaviour does this reward?" and "what will people do in response?" would have exposed the weakness before launch
- A proof of work that is easy to fake (a tail) invites faking

## Caveats

- The source is a journalist's account of a talk by Vann, and some numbers are from the narration, not Vann's own words
- Popular retellings differ on the amount paid and the scale of the catch, and the story is often grouped with a similar anecdote about cobras in British India, whose accuracy is also debated
- Vann's own book on the subject is the better source for exact figures; it was not read for this entry
