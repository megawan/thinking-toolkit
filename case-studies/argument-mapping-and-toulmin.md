# Case study: The Uses of Argument and its unexpected audience

> Documented example. It is based on the source listed below, and the details are limited to what that source reports. It is a case about the model itself, not about one team using it.

Method: [Argument Mapping and Toulmin Model](../methods/critical-thinking/argument-mapping-and-toulmin.md)

## Context

The philosopher Stephen Toulmin published *The Uses of Argument* in 1958. According to his Wikipedia biography, he wrote it while at the University of Leeds, and it examined the flaws of traditional logic. It introduced a six-part way of laying out an argument: claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier and rebuttal.

## What was done

Toulmin framed the model as a way to analyse *practical* arguments that justify a claim someone already cares about, as opposed to inferring a claim from first principles. He treated reasoning as testing and sifting existing ideas more than discovering new ones. The three essential parts were claim, grounds and warrant; qualifier, backing and rebuttal might not be needed in every argument.

## Outcome

The reception was split:

- In England, the book was poorly received. Fellow philosophers at Leeds mocked it as "Toulmin's anti-logic book"
- In the United States, rhetoricians applauded it. Wayne Brockriede and Douglas Ehninger brought it to communication scholars, who saw in it a structure for analysing and criticising rhetorical arguments

The page says the model went on to become influential in rhetoric and communication and in computer science.

## Lessons

- A model built to describe how people actually argue found its audience among people who study persuasion, not among logicians
- The most useful part for everyday arguments is the *warrant*, the rule that links evidence to claim, which is often left unspoken
- Qualifiers and rebuttals make an argument more honest: they say how sure you are and when it would not hold
- A model does not have to settle an argument to be useful; it shows where to look

## Caveats

- The source is a Wikipedia biography of Toulmin, and only part of the page was read verbatim; parts of the description rest on summaries that are not used here
- The page's own worked example is a first-person citizenship argument that it does not attribute to Toulmin
- Critics note that the model starts from a claim and does not fully represent the questions underlying an argument; this comes from material that was not read verbatim and is flagged as such
- Later uses (such as safety-case notations) were not confirmed from the verbatim text and are not described here
