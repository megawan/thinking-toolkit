# Case study: Ohno's machine that stopped

> Documented example. It is based on the sources listed below, and the details are limited to what those sources report.

Method: [Five Whys](../methods/root-cause/five-whys.md)

## Context

Five Whys is closely tied to Taiichi Ohno and the Toyota Production System. The Lean Enterprise Institute's lexicon, citing Ohno (1988, p. 17), uses his example of a machine that stopped working to show how the method works. Some retellings describe the machine as a welding robot; the Lean Enterprise Institute page says only "machine".

## What was done

The chain of questions, as the Lean Enterprise Institute page presents it:

1. Why did the machine stop? An overload blew the fuse.
2. Why was there an overload? The bearing was not lubricated enough.
3. Why was it not lubricated enough? The lubrication pump was not delivering enough.
4. Why was the pump not delivering enough? Its shaft was worn and rattling.
5. Why was the shaft worn? No strainer was fitted, so metal scraps got in.

## Outcome

The root cause was a missing strainer, not a blown fuse. The page makes the point that, without repeated whys, a manager would simply replace the fuse or the pump, and the failure would recur. The sources do not give a date, a plant, or a measured result, so this is best read as a teaching example, not as a documented improvement.

## Lessons

- Stopping at the first answer would have led to replacing the fuse
- Each answer was a checkable physical fact, not an opinion
- The chain ended at something that could be changed (fit a strainer)
- The number five is a guide: the aim is to keep asking until the root cause is reached

## Caveats

- Retellings differ in detail (machine or welding robot, wording of each step)
- Some lean practitioners argue that the example hides further questions, for example whether the first answer combines two causes. One such argument is [this lean blog post](https://www.leanblog.org/2026/04/ohno-5-whys-actually-seven/), which was not reviewed in detail for this entry.
- The Lean Enterprise Institute page gives the author and year but, in the text read for this entry, not the book title
