# Case study: Boyd, the Sabre and a contested kill ratio

> Documented example. It is based on the sources listed below, and the details are limited to what those sources report. The evidence behind this case is disputed, and the page says where.

Method: [OODA Loop](../methods/decision-making/ooda-loop.md)

## Context

John Boyd, a United States Air Force pilot and strategist, is the originator of the OODA loop. A widely told origin story links it to air combat in the Korean War between the American F-86 Sabre and the Soviet-designed MiG-15. The historian Stephen Robinson reviews that story in a 2025 article.

## What was done

According to Robinson's account of Boyd's argument:

- Boyd saw an anomaly. On paper the MiG-15 was faster, climbed better and out-accelerated the F-86, and it had a better sustained turn. Pilot experience alone did not seem to explain the Sabre's reputed success.
- He pointed to two features of the Sabre. Its bubble canopy gave better visibility, which meant better *observation* and *orientation*. Its hydraulic controls, likened to power steering, allowed quick transitions between manoeuvres, so a decision became an action faster.
- His conclusion was that American pilots cycled through the observe-orient-decide-act loop faster, so that the MiG pilots became disoriented.

Boyd cited an exchange rate of about 11 to 1 in a 1977 Air Force oral history, and "11 to 1 or somewhere between 10 to 14" in his 1978 briefing *Patterns of Conflict*. The commonly quoted official figure is 10 to 1.

## Outcome

The idea of winning by cycling through observation, orientation, decision and action faster than an opponent became central to Boyd's work and to later management thinking. But the Korean evidence has been challenged. Robinson reports:

- The 10 to 1 figure is described by him as originating from wartime propaganda, and historian Michael Hankins is quoted as calling it "almost certainly wrong" and "more likely much lower, although still in the US's favor"
- Other authors give very different figures, such as about 5.8 to 1 overall (Dildy and Thompson), 1.4 to 1 against elite Soviet units, and 1.1 to 1.5 to 1 (Cleaver)
- If the ratio is much lower, the anomaly Boyd set out to explain shrinks
- Robinson concludes that the OODA loop is not an adequate explanation for that air combat, while still finding it useful as a more general model

Boyd himself, quoted by Robinson, said that the loop came from his work on the YF-16 and YF-17 aircraft, not from Korea.

## Lessons

- The OODA loop is a model of competing decision cycles, and it is worth understanding on its own terms, whatever its origin story
- The most important stage, *orientation*, includes the picture of the world you hold; a flawed picture (here, perhaps, of how large the advantage was) leads to flawed conclusions
- A good story can outlive weak evidence; check the numbers behind a famous case
- Speed of the loop matters only when each stage is sound

## Caveats

- Boyd's Korean service is described differently by the two sources: Robinson gives 29 missions and Wikipedia gives 22 over about two months, and Wikipedia says he did not fire his guns or score a kill
- Wikipedia's account places the origin of the loop in Boyd's experience in the early 1970s, not in Korea
- This entry used the verbatim text of Robinson's article only in part; the rest of the page was not read
- No figure for the kill ratio is accepted here; the point of the case is that the figures disagree
