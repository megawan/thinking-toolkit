# Case study: The milkshake

> Documented example. It is based on the sources listed below, and the details are limited to what those sources report.

Method: [Jobs To Be Done](../methods/design-and-product/jobs-to-be-done.md)

## Context

Harvard Business School's Working Knowledge site describes how a fast-food restaurant chain, which it does not name, wanted to improve its milkshake sales. The account is told by the writer Carmen Nobel (14 February 2011) from the work of the professor Clayton Christensen and a fellow researcher.

## What was done

- The chain first tried the standard approach: it described a typical milkshake drinker, asked people who fit that profile what an ideal milkshake would be (thicker or thinner, chunkier or smoother, fruitier or more chocolatey), and acted on the answers. Sales did not improve.
- It then brought in a researcher who asked a different question: what job were customers hiring a milkshake to do?
- The researcher spent a full day in one restaurant, recording who bought milkshakes, when, and whether they drank them there. The next morning he interviewed customers as they left with a milkshake and asked what job they had hired it for.

## Outcome

The findings reported:

- About 40 percent of the milkshakes were bought first thing in the morning, by commuters, to take away
- Most of these buyers had a similar need: a long, boring commute, one free hand, and a wish for something to keep them from being hungry before late morning
- A milkshake beat alternatives such as a bagel or a doughnut because it was relatively tidy and filling, and a thick drink through a thin straw gave them something to do
- Another use was parents buying a treat for young children

The article frames the implications as what the company *could* do: make the morning shake thicker and more interesting, and offer a thinner one for the treat job. It says that firms should segment markets by the job a customer needs done, not by product or demographics. It does not report that the chain made these changes or what they did to sales.

## Lessons

- Asking customers about product features told the chain little; observing and asking about what happened told it more
- The same product served at least two different jobs at different times of day
- The real competitors were other things that do the job, such as bagels, doughnuts and bananas, not other milkshakes
- The article treats this as a way to find a job, not as proof of results

## Caveats

- The chain and the researcher are not named, and no sales results are given
- The study is told through the account of Christensen and a colleague; it is not an independent evaluation
- Some commentators dispute how the job is framed. One example is a commentary titled "Market Segmentation Is Soured By Milkshake Marketing" on [CustomerThink](https://customerthink.com/?p=51541), which appeared in search results and was not reviewed in detail for this entry.
