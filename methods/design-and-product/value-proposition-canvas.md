# Value Proposition Canvas

> Describe what your customer is trying to do, struggling with and hoping for, and show how your offer fits.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Design and product |
| **Time needed** | 1-3 hours, then testing with customers |
| **Best for** | Checking whether a product or service matches what a specific customer segment needs |

## What it is

The Value Proposition Canvas was developed by Alexander Osterwalder, Yves Pigneur and their colleagues, and is described in their book *Value Proposition Design* (2014). It has two sides.

| Side | Part | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer profile | Jobs | What the customer is trying to get done: functional, social and emotional |
| Customer profile | Pains | Bad outcomes, risks, obstacles and frustrations |
| Customer profile | Gains | Outcomes and benefits the customer wants or expects |
| Value map | Products and services | What you offer |
| Value map | Pain relievers | How your offer removes or reduces pains |
| Value map | Gain creators | How your offer produces the gains |

The aim is **fit**: your pain relievers and gain creators address the pains and gains that matter most to the customer, for the jobs they care about.

## Why it works

Teams often describe their product by its features. The canvas forces them to start from the customer and then show, item by item, how the offer helps. It exposes features nobody needs and needs that nothing addresses. Ranking pains and gains by importance keeps the focus on the few that decide the choice.

## When to use

- Designing a new product or service
- A product is not selling and you want to find out why
- Refining a message for a customer segment
- Preparing for customer interviews and experiments

## When not to use

- You have not defined a target customer; the canvas needs one segment at a time
- You want to design the whole business model; use the [Business Model and Lean Canvas](../strategy-and-risk/business-model-and-lean-canvas.md)
- You rely only on internal opinions, with no customer evidence

## How to apply

1. **Choose one customer segment.** Be specific.
2. **Fill in the customer profile** from research, using [Jobs To Be Done](jobs-to-be-done.md) and an [Empathy Map](empathy-map.md):
   - List the jobs.
   - List the pains.
   - List the gains.
3. **Rank** jobs, pains and gains by importance to the customer. Mark the top few.
4. **Fill in the value map.** List your products and services; then, for each top pain, write how you relieve it; for each top gain, how you create it.
5. **Check the fit.** Draw links between top pains and gains and the matching relievers and creators. Look for gaps (important pains with no reliever) and for waste (features that address nothing).
6. **Mark the assumptions** you are least sure about.
7. **Test them** in customer conversations or small experiments.
8. **Revise** the canvas and the offer.

## Questions to ask

| Part | Questions |
| --- | --- |
| Jobs | What is the customer trying to get done? What would they like to achieve? |
| Pains | What annoys them? What do they fear? What stops them from getting it done? |
| Gains | What would make them happy? What would exceed their expectations? |
| Pain relievers | Which pains do we remove or reduce, and how? |
| Gain creators | Which gains do we create, and how? |

## Example: a meal-planning app for busy parents

Customer profile:

| Part | Items (top ones first) |
| --- | --- |
| Jobs | Feed the family healthy meals on weekdays; avoid arguments at dinner; keep the food budget under control |
| Pains | Deciding what to cook each night; wasting food that goes off; shopping for forgotten items |
| Gains | A weekly plan in under 10 minutes; children eating without complaint; lower grocery bills |

Value map:

| Part | Items |
| --- | --- |
| Products and services | App with weekly plans, shopping list, recipes |
| Pain relievers | Suggests a week of meals in one tap; builds a shopping list; reuses leftover ingredients |
| Gain creators | Child-friendly filter; cost per meal; saves favourites |

**Fit check:** The pain "wasting food" is covered by the reuse feature. The pain "forgotten items" is solved by the list. The gain "children eating without complaint" is only partly covered.

**Next step:** Interview 10 parents about what children will eat, and test a "family favourites" feature.

## Pitfalls

- **Describing features as benefits.** Link each item to a pain or gain.
- **Assuming instead of researching.** Mark guesses and test them.
- **Too many items.** Focus on the top three of each.
- **Several segments on one canvas.** Make one canvas per segment.
- **Ignoring jobs.** Pains and gains only make sense in context.
- **Treating fit as fixed.** Customers and alternatives change.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] One customer segment
- [ ] Jobs, pains and gains from research
- [ ] Items ranked by importance
- [ ] Relievers and creators linked to the top items
- [ ] Gaps and unneeded features noted
- [ ] Riskiest assumptions tested
