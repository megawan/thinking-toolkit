# Design Thinking

> Solve problems by understanding the people affected, trying ideas quickly in rough form, and learning from their reactions.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Design and product |
| **Time needed** | A day for a quick pass; weeks for a full project |
| **Best for** | Ill-defined problems about people's experience, where the right problem is not yet clear |

## What it is

Design thinking is a human-centred approach to problem solving that comes from the practice of designers. It was developed and spread by the design firm IDEO and the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford (the d.school), building on earlier ideas about design as a way of thinking, such as Herbert Simon's *The Sciences of the Artificial* (1969).

The d.school describes five stages. They are not a strict sequence: teams loop back as they learn.

| Stage | Purpose | Typical activities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Empathise | Understand the people and their context | Interviews, observation, immersion |
| Define | State the problem you will solve | Insights, point-of-view statement, "How might we" question |
| Ideate | Generate many possible solutions | Brainstorming, sketching, combining |
| Prototype | Make ideas tangible, cheaply | Sketches, paper models, mock-ups, role play |
| Test | Learn from people using the prototype | Observation, feedback, iteration |

## Why it works

Many projects fail by building a well-made answer to the wrong problem. Design thinking starts with people and keeps checking the work against their reactions. Rough prototypes cost little, so mistakes are cheap and learning is fast. Mixing disciplines and viewpoints brings out options a single specialism would miss.

## When to use

- The problem is unclear or involves human behaviour and experience
- Existing solutions are not working and you do not know why
- A team needs to build shared understanding of users
- You need to explore before committing money

## When not to use

- The problem is well defined and the solution is a known technical fix
- You cannot reach any users
- You need a legally fixed process or a pure optimisation

## How to apply

1. **Frame the challenge.** Write a short brief: who is it for, and what area are you exploring?
2. **Empathise.** Talk to and observe 5-10 people. Ask about stories and behaviour, not just opinions. Use the [Empathy Map](empathy-map.md) to organise what you learn.
3. **Define.** Pull out the main insights and write a point-of-view statement: *[User] needs [need] because [insight]*. Turn it into a [How Might We](../ideation/how-might-we.md) question.
4. **Ideate.** Generate many ideas using methods such as [Crazy 8s](../ideation/crazy-8s.md) or [Brainwriting 6-3-5](../ideation/brainwriting-635.md). Defer judgment, then select a few.
5. **Prototype.** Build the cheapest version that can answer a question: a sketch, a paper screen, a role play, a mock-up.
6. **Test.** Put the prototype in front of users. Watch what they do and ask why. Note what worked and what did not.
7. **Iterate.** Go back to any earlier stage: refine the prototype, redefine the problem or return to users.
8. **Decide and build** when the evidence supports it.

## Principles

- Start with people, not technology
- Keep the problem open until you understand it
- Prefer making and testing to long discussions
- Welcome rough, early work
- Expect to repeat stages

## Example: improving a hospital waiting room

**Challenge:** Patients find the outpatient waiting room stressful.

| Stage | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| Empathise | Staff spent two days observing and interviewed 10 patients. Patients were anxious about not knowing how long they would wait, and where to go. |
| Define | "Anxious patients need to know what is happening because uncertainty is worse than waiting." HMW: How might we make waiting feel predictable? |
| Ideate | Ideas: a live display of wait times, text messages when it is almost your turn, floor markings, a volunteer greeter. |
| Prototype | A hand-drawn wait-time board and a script for a volunteer. No software. |
| Test | Patients read the board and asked fewer questions at the desk. The greeter helped older patients most. |
| Iterate | The team added estimated times to the board and a short sign explaining the process. |

**Result:** A low-cost change before any technology was bought.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating it as workshop theatre.** Sticky notes without real users do not help.
- **Skipping empathy to get to ideas.** The insights shape everything after.
- **Polished prototypes too early.** They invite attachment and cost time.
- **Testing with colleagues.** Use real users.
- **A straight line only.** Allow loops back.
- **No follow-through.** Plan how the learning becomes a decision.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Challenge framed and users identified
- [ ] Real users interviewed or observed
- [ ] Insights and a problem statement written
- [ ] Many ideas generated before choosing
- [ ] Cheap prototype built
- [ ] Tested with users and iterated
