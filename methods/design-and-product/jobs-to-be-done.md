# Jobs To Be Done

> People do not buy products; they hire them to make progress in a situation. Find the job, and you find what to build.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Design and product |
| **Time needed** | A few hours for interviews and synthesis, repeated over days |
| **Best for** | Understanding why customers choose, switch or stop using something, beyond demographics and features |

## What it is

Jobs To Be Done (JTBD) is a way of understanding customers by the progress they are trying to make in a particular circumstance. The "job" is not the product or the task. It is the change a person wants in their life, and the product is something they "hire" to do it.

The idea is associated with the Harvard professor Clayton Christensen. A well-known story is a fast-food chain that wanted to sell more milkshakes. Customers' age and taste did not explain the sales, but asking why people bought a milkshake in the morning did. Many were commuters who wanted something to make a long, boring drive more interesting and keep them full until lunch. The competitors were not other milkshakes, but bananas, bagels and doughnuts. Tony Ulwick developed a related method called outcome-driven innovation.

## The job statement

A job is often written in this form:

> When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].

| Part | Question | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation | What is happening? What triggered it? | When I am commuting alone for 40 minutes |
| Motivation | What do I want to do or feel? | I want something to occupy me and keep me full |
| Outcome | What does success look like? | So I can arrive alert and not hungry |

A job has three dimensions: **functional** (get something done), **emotional** (how I want to feel) and **social** (how I want to be seen).

## Why it works

Customers in the same demographic group may hire a product for different jobs, and customers with different profiles may hire it for the same one. Knowing the job shows who the real competitors are, including non-obvious alternatives, and it makes it clearer which features matter. It also explains why people switch: they find something that does the job better.

## When to use

- Deciding what to build or improve
- Understanding why customers choose or leave
- Finding competitors outside your category
- Writing positioning and messages that match real motives

## When not to use

- You have no access to customers or recent buyers
- The product is a commodity with a single, obvious job
- You need quantitative market sizing; use other methods alongside

## How to apply

1. **Choose whom to talk to.** Recent customers (bought in the last few weeks), people who switched, and people who considered it and did not buy.
2. **Interview about the story,** not opinions. Ask them to walk through the timeline: the first thought, the trigger, the search, the options, the decision, the first use.
3. **Probe the situation and the struggle.** What was going on? What was not working? What else did they try?
4. **Probe the forces.** What pushed them towards change (push of the situation, pull of the new solution), and what held them back (anxiety about the new, habit of the old)?
5. **Capture the job** in the statement format, including functional, emotional and social parts.
6. **Group similar jobs** across interviews.
7. **List the alternatives** people hired or could hire for each job, including doing nothing.
8. **Identify under-served jobs:** important to the customer, poorly done by today's options.
9. **Turn them into decisions:** features, messages, priorities and experiments.

## Interview questions

- What was happening in your life when you first thought about this?
- What were you using before? What was not working?
- What else did you consider, including doing nothing?
- What almost stopped you from buying?
- How did you decide? Who else was involved?
- What happened the first time you used it?
- What are you now able to do that you could not before?

## The four forces

| Force | Direction | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Push of the current situation | Towards change | The spreadsheet breaks every month |
| Pull of the new solution | Towards change | A tool that reports automatically |
| Anxiety about the new | Against change | Will my team learn it? Will data be lost? |
| Habit of the present | Against change | We know how the spreadsheet works |

## Example: a meal-kit service

**Interviews with 12 recent customers** revealed three jobs.

| Job | Situation | Alternatives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Help me cook a proper meal on a busy weekday without planning" | Tired after work; no ideas | Takeaway, ready meals, a pasta dish |
| "Let me feel I am a good parent who feeds the family well" | Children eating processed food | Cooking from scratch, school meals |
| "Help me try cooking something new with low risk" | Wanting to learn | Recipe books, cooking videos |

**Decisions:** Make the first job the headline: quick, no planning. Add a "30 minutes or less" filter, since that serves the first job. Add a gentle "first recipe" guide for the third job. The team stops comparing itself only with other meal kits, and also watches takeaway prices.

## Pitfalls

- **Confusing the job with the product.** "Use a drill" is not a job; "hang a picture" is closer, and "make my home feel like mine" is the deeper one.
- **Asking what they want.** Ask what happened.
- **Interviewing the wrong people.** Include those who switched or did not buy.
- **Too many jobs.** Focus on the one or two that matter most.
- **Ignoring emotional and social jobs.** They often decide the choice.
- **Stopping at insights.** Link each job to a decision.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Recent customers and switchers interviewed
- [ ] Timeline and triggers captured
- [ ] Four forces noted
- [ ] Jobs written in the statement form
- [ ] Alternatives, including doing nothing, listed
- [ ] Under-served jobs linked to decisions
