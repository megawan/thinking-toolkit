# Decision Journal

> Write down what you decided, why, and what you expect, then check later to separate good decisions from lucky or unlucky outcomes.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Reflection and learning |
| **Time needed** | 5-10 minutes per entry, and 5-10 minutes at review |
| **Best for** | Improving judgment on significant decisions that are made repeatedly or with uncertainty |

## What it is

A decision journal is a record of important decisions, made at the time you make them. It captures the situation, the options, your reasoning, your state of mind, and what you expect to happen. Later you return to compare the outcome with what you expected.

The management thinker Peter Drucker described a similar habit, called feedback analysis: whenever you make an important decision, write down what you expect to happen, and then, nine to twelve months later, compare the result with the expectation. The poker player and author Annie Duke discusses related habits in *Thinking in Bets* (2018), where she warns against "resulting": judging a decision only by its outcome.

## Why it works

Memory edits itself. After an outcome is known, we tend to believe we saw it coming (hindsight bias), and we rewrite our reasons to match. A written record made beforehand cannot be rewritten. Reviewing it shows what you got right and wrong, which signals strengths and blind spots. It also separates **decision quality** (was the reasoning sound given what I knew?) from **outcome quality** (how did it turn out?), which are not the same because luck plays a part.

## When to use

- Decisions with significant stakes, such as hiring, investments, strategy, career moves or product bets
- Decisions you make repeatedly, so you can learn patterns
- When you want to calibrate your confidence
- Decisions you will need to justify later

## When not to use

- Trivial or easily reversible choices
- When you would not review the journal; an unread journal does nothing
- When recording would delay an emergency decision (write it up afterwards, as soon as you can)

## How to apply

### At the time of the decision

1. **Date the entry,** and describe the situation and the decision to be made.
2. **List the options** you considered, including doing nothing.
3. **Record what you chose** and the main reasons.
4. **Note the key assumptions** and what you do not know.
5. **State your expected outcome** with a probability or a range, such as "70% that the launch hits target".
6. **Note your state:** tired, rushed, confident, under pressure. This helps spot patterns.
7. **Set a review date** and the signs that would tell you whether you were right.

### At the review

1. **Write what actually happened,** with facts.
2. **Compare with your expectation.** Where was it right or wrong?
3. **Separate skill from luck.** Was the reasoning sound? Did something unforeseeable occur? Would you decide the same way again with the same information?
4. **Write the lesson,** in a form you can use: a rule, a check or a question for next time.
5. **Look for patterns** across entries: recurring errors, overconfidence, areas of strength.

## Template

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Date and decision | What and when |
| Situation and context | Facts, constraints, who is involved |
| Options | The alternatives, including doing nothing |
| Choice and reasons | What I chose and why |
| Assumptions and unknowns | What must be true; what I do not know |
| Expected outcome | With probability or range |
| My state | Tired, rushed, confident, pressured |
| Review date and signals | When and what to check |
| Outcome (at review) | What happened |
| Reflection | Skill or luck; same decision again? |
| Lesson | A rule or check for next time |

## Example: hiring decision

**Entry (March):** Hire candidate A, with strong technical skills but little experience of working with customers, over candidate B, who has less technical depth but strong customer experience.

- **Reasons:** The team's biggest gap is technical, and customer skills can be taught.
- **Assumption:** Candidate A will be able to learn customer skills within six months.
- **Expected outcome:** 65% that A is performing well with customers by September.
- **State:** Under pressure to fill the role quickly.
- **Review date:** 30 September. Signs: customer feedback, manager's view.

**Review (September):**

- **Outcome:** A is excellent technically, but customers find communication difficult. Two complaints.
- **Reflection:** The 65% was too high given no evidence of customer skill. I discounted B's strengths because of time pressure. Not just bad luck.
- **Lesson:** When a role involves customers, test for that skill during hiring (a role-play) instead of assuming it can be taught. Check hiring decisions made under time pressure with a second person.

## Pitfalls

- **Writing after the outcome.** The value is in the record made beforehand.
- **Vague entries.** "I think it will go well" cannot be checked; give a number or a sign.
- **Judging by outcome alone.** A good decision can fail, and a bad one can succeed.
- **Never reviewing.** Put review dates in your calendar.
- **Too many entries.** Record only decisions that matter.
- **Hiding from the record.** Write down the reasons that embarrass you, too.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Recorded at the time of the decision
- [ ] Options, reasons and assumptions written
- [ ] Expected outcome with a probability or range
- [ ] State of mind noted
- [ ] Review date set
- [ ] At review: skill and luck separated
- [ ] A usable lesson written
