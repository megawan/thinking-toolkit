# Feynman Technique

> Explain an idea in plain language as if to a beginner, and let the gaps in your explanation show you what you do not yet understand.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Reflection and learning |
| **Time needed** | 20-45 minutes per concept |
| **Best for** | Learning a topic deeply, preparing to teach or present, and checking whether you really understand something |

## What it is

The Feynman Technique is a learning method named after the physicist Richard Feynman, who was known for his ability to explain difficult ideas simply, for example in his *Feynman Lectures on Physics* (1963). The steps as usually described are not something Feynman published as a method. They are a popular summary of his approach to learning and teaching, based on the idea that if you cannot explain something simply, you probably do not understand it well enough.

| Step | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Choose a concept | Pick one idea and write its name at the top of a page |
| 2. Explain it simply | Write or say an explanation as if to a beginner, in plain words |
| 3. Find the gaps | Note where you stumble, use jargon, or cannot say why |
| 4. Review and simplify | Go back to the source, fill the gaps, and explain again, more simply |

## Why it works

It is easy to feel that you understand something because you can recognise it, or because you can repeat the terms. Explaining from memory, in your own words, tests whether you can actually use the idea. Using simple language removes the cover that jargon provides. The gaps you find show exactly what to study next, so your time is spent on what you do not know instead of rereading what you do.

## When to use

- Learning a new subject or skill
- Preparing for an exam, talk or interview
- Checking that you understand something you will rely on
- Before teaching someone else

## When not to use

- You need to memorise facts or lists; use other techniques such as spaced repetition
- The topic needs practice more than explanation, such as a physical skill
- You have no source to check your explanation against

## How to apply

1. **Choose one concept** that is small enough to explain in a page. If it is too big, split it.
2. **Study it first,** briefly, from one or two good sources.
3. **Close the sources.** Write the name of the concept at the top of a blank page.
4. **Explain it** in plain language, as if you were teaching a curious child or a friend with no background. Use examples and analogies. Avoid jargon unless you define it.
5. **Notice the gaps.** Mark every place where you hesitate, hand-wave, use a term you cannot explain, or say "it just does".
6. **Go back to the source** to fill each gap. Look for the reason, not only the fact.
7. **Rewrite the explanation** shorter and simpler. Replace jargon with everyday words.
8. **Test it on a real person** if you can. Ask them to say it back or to ask questions. Their confusion shows what is still unclear.
9. **Repeat later.** Return after a few days to check what you still remember.

## Tips

- Write by hand or speak aloud; thinking in your head hides gaps
- Use concrete examples and analogies
- Ask yourself "why?" and "what is an example?" for each statement
- Draw a diagram if it makes the idea clearer
- Keep the final one-page explanation as a note

## Example: explaining compound interest

**First attempt:** "Compound interest is when interest is calculated on the principal and on accumulated interest."

**Gaps found:** I cannot say why it grows faster than simple interest without a formula. I use "principal" without saying what it is.

**After review, second attempt:** "Imagine you put $100 in a bank that pays 10% a year. After one year you have $110. In the second year, the bank pays 10% on $110, not on $100, so you earn $11, not $10. Each year the interest itself earns interest. So the growth gets bigger each year, like a snowball getting larger as it rolls. With simple interest, you always get $10, because it only counts your first $100."

**Test:** A friend repeats it and asks, "What happens after 20 years?" The author works out that $100 grows to about $673, while simple interest gives $300, and adds the numbers to the note.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading the source while explaining.** Close it, or you will not see the gaps.
- **Using jargon as a shortcut.** If you cannot define it, you do not own it.
- **Explaining too much at once.** Narrow the concept.
- **Stopping at the first explanation.** Simplify again.
- **Mistaking fluency for understanding.** Test with a real listener or with examples.
- **No later review.** Without revisiting, much will fade.
