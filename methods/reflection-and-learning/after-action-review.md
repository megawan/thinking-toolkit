# After Action Review

> Right after an event, compare what was supposed to happen with what did, and learn why.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Reflection and learning |
| **Time needed** | 10-30 minutes, as soon as possible after the event |
| **Best for** | Learning from a specific event, task, meeting, launch or incident while memory is fresh |

## What it is

An After Action Review (AAR) is a short, structured discussion of an event that asks four questions. It was developed by the United States Army in the 1970s and is described in the Army's training circular *A Leader's Guide to After-Action Reviews* (TC 25-20, 1993). Since then it has been adopted by emergency services, hospitals, businesses and non-profit organisations.

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| What did we expect to happen? | State the intent and the plan |
| What actually happened? | Establish the facts |
| Why was there a difference? | Find the causes, good and bad |
| What can we learn, and what will we do? | Turn the review into action |

Some versions add a fifth step: what will we sustain, and what will we improve?

## Why it works

Memory fades and gets rewritten quickly, and people tend to remember what supports their view. A quick review while the event is fresh captures details. Comparing the plan with the result gives a neutral starting point that does not depend on opinion. Reviewing successes as well as failures spreads good practice and prevents a "lucky" result from being taken as proof that the plan was good.

## When to use

- After a project milestone, event, presentation, launch or exercise
- After an incident or near miss
- After a routine task done by a team, to build learning into the daily work
- Whenever you want a fast learning loop

## When not to use

- The event is still unfolding (do it as soon as it ends)
- You need to examine a long period or team culture; use a [Retrospective](retrospective.md)
- The goal is to assess an individual's performance; AAR is about learning, not appraisal

## How to apply

1. **Do it soon.** Within a day if possible, while memory is fresh.
2. **Invite those involved,** including those who did the work and not only the leaders. Rank should not matter.
3. **Set the ground rules.** The aim is to learn; no blame; everyone speaks; what is said is about the event, not about the person.
4. **Ask: what did we expect to happen?** Restate the objective and the plan. Capture the measures of success.
5. **Ask: what actually happened?** Build a timeline of facts. Use records where possible.
6. **Ask: why the difference?** Discuss reasons for what went well and what did not. Dig below the first answer; [Five Whys](../root-cause/five-whys.md) helps.
7. **Ask: what can we learn?** Identify lessons that apply to the next time. Separate the controllable from the uncontrollable.
8. **Decide actions.** What will we sustain and what will we improve? Assign owners.
9. **Record and share** the lessons so others can use them, and check the actions at the next occasion.

## Tips

- Keep it short and focused on one event
- Let the people who did the work speak first
- A facilitator, not the leader, can run it, so the leader can also be questioned
- Write lessons in a form that someone who was not there can use
- Do it for successes, too

## Example: a customer webinar

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| What did we expect? | 300 registrations, 150 live attendees, and a smooth demo |
| What happened? | 280 registered, 90 attended, and the demo froze for two minutes |
| Why the difference? | Reminder emails went out only the night before; the demo ran on a laptop with a weak connection; the best-received part was the customer story |
| What can we learn? | Send reminders a day and an hour before; run demos from a recorded backup; feature a customer story earlier |

**Actions:**

- Set up two reminder emails (owner: marketing, before next webinar)
- Prepare a recorded demo as a fallback (owner: product, this week)
- Add a customer story to the start of the next agenda (owner: host)

**Sustain:** The customer story and the Q&A format.

## Pitfalls

- **Waiting too long.** Detail disappears within days.
- **Blame.** If people become defensive, the learning stops.
- **Only after failure.** Review successes too.
- **A boss's monologue.** Hear from those who did the work.
- **Vague lessons.** "Communicate better" is not usable.
- **No follow-up.** Check that the actions were done.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Held soon after the event
- [ ] Those who did the work present
- [ ] Ground rules stated
- [ ] Expected vs actual compared with facts
- [ ] Causes explored
- [ ] Specific lessons and actions recorded
- [ ] Lessons shared
