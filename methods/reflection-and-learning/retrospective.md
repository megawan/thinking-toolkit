# Retrospective

> Stop at regular intervals, look back at how the work went, and agree on a few changes for next time.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Reflection and learning |
| **Time needed** | 45-90 minutes at the end of a sprint, project phase or release |
| **Best for** | Teams that want to keep improving how they work together |

## What it is

A retrospective is a structured meeting in which a team looks back at a period of work, discusses what went well and what did not, and decides on specific improvements. The practice was described in Norman Kerth's *Project Retrospectives* (2001), and became a regular part of agile software development: one of the principles of the Agile Manifesto is that the team reflects at regular intervals on how to become more effective and adjusts accordingly. It is now used well beyond software.

Esther Derby and Diana Larsen, in *Agile Retrospectives* (2006), describe a five-phase structure.

| Phase | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Set the stage | Welcome everyone, state the goal, and create a safe tone |
| Gather data | Build a shared picture of what happened: events, metrics, feelings |
| Generate insights | Ask why things happened and look for patterns |
| Decide what to do | Choose a small number of actions with owners |
| Close | Summarise, check commitments and thank people |

## The prime directive

Kerth proposed opening with a statement of mindset:

> Regardless of what we discover, we understand and truly believe that everyone did the best job they could, given what they knew at the time, their skills and abilities, the resources available, and the situation at hand.

It sets the tone: the aim is to improve the system, not to find someone to blame.

## Why it works

Without a pause for reflection, teams repeat the same problems because they are too busy to examine them. Regular, short retrospectives turn small frustrations into small changes before they grow. Everyone has a say, so the team owns the changes. Tracking actions across sessions builds a habit of learning.

## When to use

- At the end of a sprint, project, release or event
- After a difficult period or an incident
- When a team's work does not feel as smooth as it could
- When a new team is forming

## When not to use

- There is no safety to speak openly, and the manager will punish honesty; fix that first
- The team cannot or will not act on the results
- You need to analyse a specific failure in depth; use [Five Whys](../root-cause/five-whys.md) or an [After Action Review](after-action-review.md)

## Common formats

| Format | Prompts | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start / Stop / Continue | What should we start doing? Stop doing? Keep doing? | A simple, quick default |
| 4Ls | Liked, Learned, Lacked, Longed for | Emphasising learning and unmet needs |
| Mad / Sad / Glad | What made us mad, sad, glad? | Surfacing feelings |
| Sailboat | Wind (what helps), anchors (what holds us back), rocks (risks), island (the goal) | A visual, team-goal view |
| Timeline | Mark events and emotions along the period | Long periods, where memory is patchy |

The 4Ls format is attributed to Mary Gorman and Ellen Gottesdiener. Vary the format every few sessions to keep it fresh.

## How to apply

1. **Prepare.** Choose the period, the facilitator and a format. Collect facts such as dates, metrics and incidents.
2. **Set the stage (5 minutes).** State the goal and the prime directive. Agree on confidentiality: what is said here stays here, but actions are shared.
3. **Gather data (15 minutes).** Everyone writes notes silently, one idea per note, using the format's prompts. Post them on a wall or board.
4. **Group the notes** by theme, and name each group.
5. **Generate insights (15 minutes).** For the biggest themes, ask why. What patterns do you see? What is in our control?
6. **Vote** on the one to three themes worth working on.
7. **Decide what to do (15 minutes).** For each, define a specific action: what, who and by when. Keep it small enough to try in the next period.
8. **Close (5 minutes).** Read back the actions. Ask each person for a word on how the session went.
9. **Follow up.** Start the next retrospective by checking last time's actions.

## Example: a software team after a sprint

**Format:** Start / Stop / Continue.

| Column | Notes raised |
| --- | --- |
| Start | Short daily check on blocked tasks; pairing on risky changes |
| Stop | Adding work in the middle of the sprint; long status meetings |
| Continue | Demo to customers at the end of each sprint; shared on-call rota |

**Insight:** Unplanned work in mid-sprint was the biggest cause of missed goals.

**Actions:**

- Agree a rule that new requests go to the next sprint unless the product owner swaps something out (owner: product owner, from next sprint)
- Replace the long status meeting with a 10-minute standing check (owner: team lead, this week)

**Next retrospective:** The team checks both actions first. Fewer mid-sprint additions, and the meeting is shorter.

## Pitfalls

- **Complaint without action.** End with specific, owned actions.
- **Too many actions.** One to three are enough.
- **The same format every time.** Change it to keep attention.
- **Blame.** Return to the prime directive.
- **A manager who dominates.** Let the team lead the discussion; use silent writing first.
- **No follow-up.** Open the next session with the actions.
- **Skipping it when busy.** Busy periods need it most.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Goal and prime directive stated
- [ ] Data gathered silently first
- [ ] Notes grouped and themes named
- [ ] Insights discussed, not just symptoms
- [ ] One to three actions with owners and dates
- [ ] Follow-up planned for the next session
