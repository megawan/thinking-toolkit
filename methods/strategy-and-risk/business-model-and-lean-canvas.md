# Business Model and Lean Canvas

> Describe a whole business on one page, with nine blocks, so you can test and change it quickly.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Strategy and risk |
| **Time needed** | 1-2 hours for a first draft; repeated revisions as you learn |
| **Best for** | Planning a new venture, understanding an existing business, and finding the riskiest assumptions |

## What it is

Two one-page canvases share the same idea.

The **Business Model Canvas** was created by Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur and described in *Business Model Generation* (2010). It has nine blocks.

The **Lean Canvas** was created by Ash Maurya in about 2010, adapted from the Business Model Canvas for start-ups facing high uncertainty, and described in *Running Lean*. It replaces some blocks with ones that focus on problems and measures.

| Business Model Canvas | Lean Canvas |
| --- | --- |
| Customer segments | Customer segments |
| Value propositions | Unique value proposition |
| Channels | Channels |
| Customer relationships | Problem |
| Revenue streams | Revenue streams |
| Key resources | Solution |
| Key activities | Key metrics |
| Key partnerships | Unfair advantage |
| Cost structure | Cost structure |

## What each block asks

| Block | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Customer segments | Who are we creating value for? Who is the first customer? |
| Value proposition / Unique value proposition | What value do we deliver, and why is it different and worth buying? |
| Problem (Lean) | What are the top 1-3 problems, and how are they solved today? |
| Solution (Lean) | What is the simplest solution for each problem? |
| Channels | How do we reach and serve customers? |
| Customer relationships | What relationship does each segment expect? |
| Revenue streams | What do customers pay for, and how? |
| Key resources | What assets do we need? |
| Key activities | What must we do well? |
| Key partnerships | Who are our key partners and suppliers? |
| Key metrics (Lean) | Which numbers tell us whether the model is working? |
| Unfair advantage (Lean) | What cannot be easily copied or bought? |
| Cost structure | What are the biggest costs? |

## Why it works

A business plan of 40 pages takes weeks and is out of date quickly. A one-page canvas can be drafted in an hour, shared, challenged and changed. Seeing all the blocks together shows how they depend on each other: the value proposition needs the right channel; revenue must cover costs. It makes assumptions visible so they can be tested.

## When to use

- Starting a new product, service or business
- Explaining a business to others
- Analysing a competitor or an existing company
- Planning a change to a business model

## When not to use

- You need detailed financial forecasts
- The problem is operational and does not touch the business model
- You have not defined the customer segment; start with the [Value Proposition Canvas](../design-and-product/value-proposition-canvas.md)

## How to apply

1. **Pick the canvas.** Use the Lean Canvas for an early-stage idea with a problem to prove; use the Business Model Canvas for an existing or more mature business.
2. **Draw the canvas** on a large sheet or whiteboard, with sticky notes.
3. **Start with customers and the problem or value.** Who has the problem and what do they get?
4. **Fill the rest** in the order that makes sense: channels, revenue, costs, then resources, activities and partners (or solution, metrics and advantage).
5. **Write short phrases,** one idea per note. Fill in only what you know; mark guesses.
6. **Make versions for different segments** if they differ much.
7. **Check coherence.** Does each block support the others? Do revenues exceed costs? Can the channels reach the segment?
8. **Mark the riskiest assumptions** and rank them.
9. **Test them** with interviews, prototypes or small experiments, starting with the riskiest.
10. **Update the canvas** after each test.

## Example: a neighbourhood repair cafe (Lean Canvas)

| Block | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Customer segments | Residents who want to repair broken items; first customers: older residents with small appliances |
| Problem | Repairs cost more than replacement; people do not know who can help; waste |
| Unique value proposition | Fix it with neighbours, cheap and friendly, and learn to repair |
| Solution | A monthly repair day with volunteer repairers and tool sharing |
| Channels | Local noticeboards, community group, word of mouth |
| Revenue streams | Voluntary donations; small fee for parts; sponsorship from local shops |
| Cost structure | Venue hire, tools, insurance, parts |
| Key metrics | Items repaired per session, return visitors, donations per session |
| Unfair advantage | Volunteers with skills; trusted by the community |

**Riskiest assumption:** Enough skilled volunteers will come every month. **Test:** Ask 20 local tradespeople and hobbyists to commit to one session each, before booking a venue.

## Pitfalls

- **A canvas with guesses taken as facts.** Mark assumptions and test them.
- **Too many segments.** Start with one.
- **Vague value proposition.** Say what is different and why it matters.
- **Never updating.** Revise after every learning step.
- **Ignoring costs.** Check that the model can make money or cover its costs.
- **Filling the canvas once.** It is a tool for testing, not a document to file.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Canvas chosen to fit the stage
- [ ] One customer segment first
- [ ] Short phrases, assumptions marked
- [ ] Blocks checked for coherence
- [ ] Riskiest assumptions ranked
- [ ] First test planned
