# Porter's Five Forces

> Judge how attractive an industry is by looking at five pressures on its profits.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Strategy and risk |
| **Time needed** | 1-3 hours, plus research |
| **Best for** | Entering or staying in an industry, setting strategy, and finding where competitive pressure comes from |

## What it is

Porter's Five Forces is a framework for analysing the competitive structure of an industry. It was introduced by Michael Porter, a professor at Harvard Business School, in the *Harvard Business Review* in 1979 and revisited in 2008. It argues that industry profitability depends on five forces, not only on direct rivals.

| Force | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Threat of new entrants | How easy is it for new competitors to enter? |
| Bargaining power of suppliers | How much can suppliers raise prices or reduce quality? |
| Bargaining power of buyers | How much can customers push prices down or demand more? |
| Threat of substitutes | Can customers meet the same need another way? |
| Rivalry among existing competitors | How intense is competition between current firms? |

The stronger the forces, the lower the profit potential of the industry.

## Why it works

Firms often focus on the competitors they can see. The framework widens the view to suppliers, customers, substitutes and new entrants, which together determine who captures the value. It helps find where to position, which pressures to defend against, and which industries to avoid.

## When to use

- Deciding whether to enter or invest in an industry
- Reviewing strategy and positioning
- Understanding why profits in a sector are thin
- Preparing a business plan or an investment case

## When not to use

- A fast-changing situation where industry boundaries are unclear; combine with scenario thinking
- A single small decision
- As a list of facts: the value is in judging each force and drawing conclusions

## How to apply

1. **Define the industry** and the scope: the product, the geography and the customer segment.
2. **Analyse each force,** using the factors below.
3. **Rate each force** as low, medium or high, with reasons.
4. **Look at the whole.** Which forces are strongest? Which matter most for profit?
5. **Compare** with the industry's past or with other industries.
6. **Consider trends.** Which forces are getting stronger or weaker?
7. **Draw conclusions.** Where can the firm position itself to reduce the pressure? What should it avoid?
8. **Decide actions,** such as differentiating, locking in suppliers or entering an adjacent niche.
9. **Revisit** when the industry changes.

## Factors to consider

| Force | Higher force when... | Lower force when... |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New entrants | Low capital needs, no brand loyalty, easy access to channels | High capital needs, strong brands, scale advantages, regulation, switching costs |
| Suppliers | Few suppliers, unique inputs, high switching costs for you | Many suppliers, standard inputs, easy to switch |
| Buyers | Few large buyers, products are standard, low switching costs | Many small buyers, differentiated products, high switching costs |
| Substitutes | Alternatives are cheap or better, easy to switch | No close alternatives, high switching costs |
| Rivalry | Many equal competitors, slow growth, high fixed costs, little differentiation | Few rivals, fast growth, differentiated offers |

## Example: an independent online bookshop

| Force | Rating | Reasons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New entrants | High | Setting up a shop is cheap; big platforms can add books easily |
| Suppliers | Medium | A few distributors dominate, but several exist; small shops have little leverage on terms |
| Buyers | High | Customers compare prices instantly and switch at no cost |
| Substitutes | High | E-books, audiobooks, libraries and second-hand markets |
| Rivalry | High | Large platforms compete on price and delivery speed |

**Conclusion:** The industry is hard for a general bookshop. Pressure is lower in niches: signed or rare editions, a curated list for one community, events and subscriptions. The shop decides to specialise in local-interest titles with author events, where buyers have fewer substitutes and loyalty is higher.

## Pitfalls

- **A list without judgment.** Rate each force and explain.
- **Too broad an industry.** Segment it; forces differ by segment.
- **Ignoring complements.** Some analysts add a sixth force, complementary products.
- **Static view.** Forces change over time.
- **Only for large firms.** Small businesses can use it too.
- **No action.** Finish with positioning decisions.
