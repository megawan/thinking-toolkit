# Ladder of Inference

> Trace how you move from raw facts to conclusions and actions, and check each rung for steps you took without noticing.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 10-30 minutes |
| **Best for** | Disagreements, misunderstandings and decisions based on a quick judgment of someone or something |

## What it is

The ladder of inference is a model by the organisational psychologist Chris Argyris, popularised by Peter Senge in *The Fifth Discipline Fieldbook* (1994). It describes the mental steps we climb, usually in a split second, from what happened to what we do about it.

| Rung | Description | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Observable data | What a camera would record | The colleague did not speak in the meeting |
| 2. Selected data | The parts we notice | We notice the silence and miss that they took notes |
| 3. Added meaning | Interpretations from culture and context | "They are uninterested" |
| 4. Assumptions | Beliefs we take as given | "People who are uninterested do not care about the work" |
| 5. Conclusions | What we decide is true | "They are not committed to the project" |
| 6. Beliefs | Lasting views about the world | "Some people don't pull their weight" |
| 7. Actions | What we do | Leave them out of the next planning meeting |

Because we climb quickly, the process feels like seeing the facts. Our beliefs then affect which data we select next, so the loop reinforces itself.

## Why it works

Disagreements are often not about the data at the bottom, but about the rungs we did not know we had climbed. Slowing down and walking down the ladder separates fact from interpretation, shows where two people's paths diverged, and allows a conversation about the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

## When to use

- You feel strongly certain about someone's motives
- Two people look at the same events and draw different conclusions
- A conflict feels stuck
- You are about to make a decision based on a quick impression

## When not to use

- A fast decision in an emergency where there is no time
- Simple facts that anyone can verify
- When using it would make the conversation feel like an examination of the other person

## How to apply

1. **Notice your conclusion** or a reaction. Ask: "What am I concluding, and how sure am I?"
2. **Walk down the ladder.** What belief, assumption and meaning led to it?
3. **Return to the observable data.** What did I actually see and hear? What would a camera show?
4. **Ask what you left out.** What data did you not select? What else could explain it?
5. **Test the assumptions.** Which are facts and which are guesses?
6. **List alternative interpretations.** At least two other meanings that fit the same data.
7. **Check with the other person,** using inquiry: "Here is what I noticed. Here is what I made of it. How do you see it?"
8. **Share your own reasoning openly** when you speak: state the data, your interpretation and your conclusion, then invite a challenge.
9. **Choose the action** after the check, not before.

## Phrases that help

| Purpose | Say |
| --- | --- |
| Share your ladder | "What I noticed was... I took that to mean... so I concluded..." |
| Ask for theirs | "What did you see that led you to that view?" |
| Test an assumption | "I'm assuming X. Does that match your experience?" |
| Slow down | "Let me check that I have the facts right." |

## Example: a missed deadline

**Observation (data):** A teammate delivered the report two days late.

| Rung | Without checking | After walking down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selected data | Late delivery; no message | Also: they worked on a client emergency that week (I had heard, but did not connect it) |
| Meaning | "They don't care about deadlines" | "They were stretched" |
| Assumption | "People who care tell you early" | "They may not have known how to raise it" |
| Conclusion | "They are unreliable" | "They need support and clearer escalation" |
| Action | Stop giving them important work | Ask what happened; agree how to flag problems early |

**Conversation:** "The report arrived on Thursday, and I had expected Tuesday. I wondered if something was getting in the way. What happened?" They explained the client emergency and agreed to message earlier next time.

## Pitfalls

- **Using it to analyse others.** Start with your own ladder.
- **Treating all interpretations as equal.** Some are better supported than others.
- **Pretending there is no inference.** We cannot act without it; the aim is to make it visible.
- **A therapy tone.** Keep it practical and respectful.
- **Skipping the data rung.** If you cannot say what a camera would show, you are already up the ladder.
- **Stopping at understanding.** Decide what to do next.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Conclusion noticed
- [ ] Walked down to observable data
- [ ] What was not selected considered
- [ ] Assumptions marked as fact or guess
- [ ] Alternative interpretations listed
- [ ] Checked with the other person
- [ ] Action chosen after the check
