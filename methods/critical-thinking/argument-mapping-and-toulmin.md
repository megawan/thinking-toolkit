# Argument Mapping and Toulmin Model

> Lay out an argument as claim, evidence and the link between them, so you can see what holds it up and where it is weak.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 15-45 minutes per argument |
| **Best for** | Analysing a proposal, essay or debate point, and building a clear case of your own |

## What it is

Argument mapping is a way of drawing the structure of an argument: which statements support which, and which object. The **Toulmin model** is a widely used layout, from the philosopher Stephen Toulmin's book *The Uses of Argument* (1958). It breaks a practical argument into six parts.

| Part | Meaning | Question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Claim | The conclusion whose merit has to be established | What are you asking me to accept? |
| Grounds (data) | The facts or evidence the claim rests on | What do you base it on? |
| Warrant | The statement that authorises the move from grounds to claim | Why do these grounds support this claim? |
| Backing | What certifies the warrant | Why should I trust that link? |
| Qualifier | Words showing how strong the claim is ("probably", "presumably") | How certain is it? |
| Rebuttal | The conditions under which the claim would not hold | When would it fail? |

Claim, grounds and warrant are the essential parts. Qualifier, backing and rebuttal are not always needed. Toulmin saw reasoning as testing and sifting ideas more than discovering new ones, and his model was built for everyday, practical arguments, not formal proofs.

## Why it works

Most weak arguments fail at the warrant: the link between evidence and claim is assumed and never stated. Writing the six parts forces the warrant into the open, where it can be tested. Separating claim, grounds and rebuttal also makes it easier to see which part to challenge, and which part a critic is actually attacking.

## When to use

- Reviewing a proposal or business case
- Preparing an essay, a pitch or a policy brief
- Settling a disagreement by locating where the two sides differ
- Teaching or practising clear reasoning

## When not to use

- Formal logic or mathematical proof, where stricter tools exist
- Quick informal chat
- When the question is about what to ask, not what is claimed; the model starts from a claim

## How to apply

1. **State the claim** in one sentence. What exactly is being argued?
2. **List the grounds:** the facts, data or evidence offered. Note their source and quality.
3. **Find the warrant.** Ask "why does this evidence mean the claim is true?" Write the general rule that links them. It is often unstated.
4. **Look for backing** for the warrant: laws, research, experience or definitions that show the rule holds.
5. **Add the qualifier.** How strong is the claim: always, usually, probably?
6. **List rebuttals:** conditions under which the warrant fails or the claim does not hold.
7. **Test each link.** Are the grounds true? Is the warrant plausible? Is the backing solid? Do rebuttals apply here?
8. **Decide.** Accept, accept in a weaker form, ask for more, or reject.
9. **Redraw your own argument** the same way before you present it.

## Drawing a map

An argument map is a diagram with the claim at the top and supporting statements below, linked by arrows. Objections are drawn in a different colour. Several premises that work together are joined. Paper, a whiteboard or any diagram tool is enough.

```text
Claim: We should move customer support to a four-day rota
   ^
   |-- Grounds: Support tickets peak Tuesday to Thursday
   |-- Warrant: Staffing should follow demand
   |-- Backing: Last year's ticket data
   '-- Rebuttal: Unless contracts promise Monday and Friday cover
```

## Example: a standard textbook argument

| Part | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Claim | "I am a British citizen." |
| Grounds | "I was born in Bermuda." |
| Warrant | "A person born in Bermuda is, as a rule, a British citizen." |
| Backing | The relevant nationality law |
| Qualifier | "Presumably" |
| Rebuttal | "Unless the person has since given up the citizenship" |

**What the structure shows:** If someone challenges the claim, the discussion can go to the right place: are the grounds true (was the person born there)? Is the warrant general enough? Does a rebuttal apply?

## Example: a workplace proposal

**Claim:** "We should hire a second data analyst."

| Part | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Grounds | The report queue has grown from 3 to 12 weeks |
| Warrant | When a backlog results from limited capacity, adding capacity reduces it |
| Backing | When the team added a contractor last year, the queue fell |
| Qualifier | "Probably" |
| Rebuttal | Unless the delay comes from slow data access, not analyst time |

**Test:** The reviewer asks whether the delay is caused by analysts or by data access. The rebuttal is the question to settle before the hire.

## Pitfalls

- **Missing the warrant.** Without it, you only have two facts side by side.
- **Mapping only your own side.** Include the strongest objections.
- **Treating the model as a verdict.** It organises the argument; it does not decide it.
- **Too much detail.** Keep each box to a sentence.
- **Confusing evidence with claim.** A claim reported by many people is still a claim.
- **Ignoring the question underneath.** The model starts at a claim; ask what question it answers.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Claim stated in one sentence
- [ ] Grounds listed with sources
- [ ] Warrant written out
- [ ] Backing and qualifier added
- [ ] Rebuttals listed
- [ ] Each link tested
- [ ] Own argument mapped too
