# Socratic Questioning

> Examine a claim by asking disciplined questions about what it means, what supports it and what follows from it.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 15-45 minutes |
| **Best for** | Testing ideas, coaching, teaching, and examining your own assumptions |

## What it is

Socratic questioning is a way of thinking and teaching through questions, named after Socrates, whose method appears in the dialogues written by Plato. Instead of telling someone the answer, you ask questions that help them clarify their own thinking and find the gaps in it.

The educators Richard Paul and Linda Elder describe six types of Socratic questions.

| Type | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clarification | Understand what is meant | What do you mean by...? Can you give an example? |
| Probing assumptions | Surface what is taken for granted | What are you assuming? What if it were not so? |
| Probing reasons and evidence | Test the support | How do you know? What evidence is there? |
| Viewpoints and perspectives | Consider other angles | How would someone who disagrees see it? |
| Implications and consequences | Follow the idea forward | If that were true, what follows? What would happen next? |
| Questions about the question | Reflect on the inquiry | Why is this question important? Is there a better question? |

## Why it works

Telling invites agreement or defence. Asking invites thinking. People are more likely to accept and remember conclusions they reach themselves. Questions also reveal assumptions that the speaker has never examined, and that the questioner might have shared.

## When to use

- Reviewing a plan, claim or decision
- Coaching or teaching, when you want someone to think it through
- Examining your own beliefs
- A discussion where people use the same words for different things

## When not to use

- A quick factual answer is needed
- There is time pressure or an emergency
- It becomes an interrogation; the other person must feel safe

## How to apply

1. **Set the tone.** Tell the person that you want to understand and test the idea together, not to catch them out.
2. **Start with clarification.** Make sure you both understand the claim.
3. **Probe assumptions** one at a time.
4. **Ask for reasons and evidence.** What supports this? How reliable is it?
5. **Explore other viewpoints.** What would a critic say? What would an expert in another field say?
6. **Follow the implications.** If it is true, what else should be true? What are the consequences?
7. **Reflect.** What have we learned? Does the claim stand, need changing or need dropping?
8. **Summarise** in their words and agree on next steps.

## Good practice

- Ask one question at a time, then listen
- Use open questions that begin with what, how, why or when
- Paraphrase the answer before the next question
- Stay curious; the goal is not to win
- Allow silence for people to think

## Example 1: reviewing a plan to open a second shop

| Type | Question and answer |
| --- | --- |
| Clarification | "What does a successful second shop look like?" "Half the first shop's sales within a year." |
| Assumptions | "What are you assuming about the new area?" "That it has the same customer mix." |
| Evidence | "How do you know?" "I have not checked, but it is close to a university." |
| Viewpoints | "How would a competitor there see your entry?" "They might cut prices." |
| Implications | "If sales are lower, what happens to the first shop?" "I would divert staff and stock." |
| About the question | "Is opening a second shop the real question?" "Maybe it is how to grow sales." |

**Result:** The owner decides to test the new area with a stall for a month, and also to look at online sales.

## Example 2: examining your own belief

**Belief:** "I am bad at public speaking."

- What do I mean by bad? (I get nervous and speed up.)
- What evidence do I have? (Two bad talks, years ago.)
- Is there evidence against it? (A well-received team update last month.)
- What would I tell a friend in the same position? (To practise and get feedback.)

**Result:** The belief becomes "I get nervous and need practice", which is a problem with a solution.

## Pitfalls

- **Leading questions.** "Don't you think that is risky?" hides the answer in the question.
- **Too many questions at once.** One at a time.
- **Aggression.** A "gotcha" tone ends the conversation.
- **Never concluding.** Summarise and decide.
- **Questions with no listening.** Respond to the answers.
- **Using it to avoid taking a position.** Say what you think when asked.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Tone set as shared inquiry
- [ ] Claim clarified first
- [ ] Assumptions and evidence probed
- [ ] Other viewpoints considered
- [ ] Implications followed
- [ ] Summary and next steps agreed
