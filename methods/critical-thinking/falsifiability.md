# Falsifiability

> Ask what result would prove you wrong; a claim that no evidence could ever contradict tells you little.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 10-30 minutes |
| **Best for** | Testing hypotheses, forecasts and business beliefs, and designing tests that can teach you something |

## What it is

A claim is falsifiable if it is possible, at least in principle, to imagine an observation that would show it to be false. The idea is associated with the philosopher Karl Popper, who proposed it in *Logik der Forschung* (1934; English edition *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, 1959) as a criterion for separating scientific claims from non-scientific ones.

| Claim | Falsifiable? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "All swans are white." | Yes | One black swan would refute it |
| "This price change will raise sales by at least 5% within two months." | Yes | The sales figures will show it |
| "Things happen for a reason." | No | No observation could contradict it |
| "Our customers will like the product, one way or another." | No | Any outcome can be read as agreement |

Popper stressed **risky predictions**: a hypothesis that forbids many outcomes and survives a real chance of failure deserves more confidence than one that could explain anything. He contrasted this with theories that fit every observation because they exclude nothing.

## Why it works

A belief that can explain any outcome cannot be tested, so it cannot be improved. Asking "what would change my mind?" forces a hypothesis into a form where evidence can bear on it. It also guards against confirmation bias: instead of collecting examples that agree, you look for the one that would not.

## When to use

- Setting up a test, pilot or A/B experiment
- Judging a forecast or strategy that seems to be "always right"
- Reviewing a claim that comes with many excuses
- Stating your own beliefs more precisely

## When not to use

- Questions of value or taste, which are not tested by observation
- As a rigid rule for all knowledge: critics argue that real science is more complicated (see below)
- When no test is feasible; say so openly instead of pretending

## How to apply

1. **State the hypothesis** in a clear sentence.
2. **Ask: what would count against it?** Describe an observation that would show it to be wrong.
3. **Make it specific.** Add numbers, time frames and conditions: how much, by when, for whom.
4. **Make it risky.** Prefer predictions that could easily fail if you were wrong.
5. **Agree the criteria in advance.** Decide before the test what result counts as support and what counts as failure.
6. **Run the test** and record the result.
7. **Respond honestly.** If the prediction fails, revise or drop the hypothesis. Avoid rescuing it with an excuse that makes it untestable.
8. **Check the background assumptions.** A failed test may point at the hypothesis or at the test itself.

## Example: a marketing belief

**Belief:** "Our newsletter builds loyalty."

| Version | Falsifiable? |
| --- | --- |
| "The newsletter builds loyalty." | Barely; any result can be explained |
| "Customers who read the newsletter reorder more often than similar customers who do not." | Yes |
| "Over three months, newsletter readers will place at least 10% more repeat orders than a matched group of non-readers." | Yes, and risky |

**Test and result:** The matched comparison shows 2% more. The belief, in its precise form, failed. The team asks what the newsletter does achieve (perhaps announcements) and changes its goals.

## A historical example

In 1919, expeditions led by Arthur Eddington and others photographed stars near the Sun during a solar eclipse, to test a prediction of Einstein's general relativity: that the Sun's gravity would bend starlight by about 1.75 arcseconds, twice the value of a Newtonian-style calculation (about 0.83). The measurements could have contradicted general relativity. This kind of risky, checkable prediction is what Popper valued.

## Limits of the idea

| Criticism | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Duhem-Quine thesis | Testing a hypothesis always depends on background assumptions, so a failed test never isolates the hypothesis alone |
| Lakatos and Kuhn | Real science often keeps a theory in the face of anomalies for good reasons; refutation is less clean than the simple picture |
| Dogmatic use | Treating one failed test as final can be as unwise as ignoring it |

Use falsifiability as a discipline for making claims testable, not as a mechanical rule that settles every question.

## Pitfalls

- **Unfalsifiable wording.** "Might", "in some way" and "eventually" protect a claim from evidence.
- **Moving the goalposts.** Redefining success after the result is known.
- **Ad hoc rescue.** Adding a special excuse to save a failing idea.
- **Confirmation only.** Counting supporting examples while ignoring the test that matters.
- **Testing too gently.** A test the hypothesis cannot fail teaches nothing.
- **Over-trusting one result.** Check assumptions, and replicate if you can.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Hypothesis stated clearly
- [ ] Failure condition described
- [ ] Numbers, time frame and conditions added
- [ ] Prediction is risky
- [ ] Success and failure criteria agreed in advance
- [ ] Result accepted honestly
- [ ] Background assumptions reviewed
