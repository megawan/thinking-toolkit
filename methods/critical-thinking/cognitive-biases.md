# Cognitive Biases

> Know the common ways the mind is led astray, and build simple habits that counter them.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 10-30 minutes as a check on a decision |
| **Best for** | Reviewing important judgments, forecasts and decisions for predictable errors |

## What it is

A cognitive bias is a systematic pattern in how people judge and decide that departs from what careful reasoning would give. The study of these patterns grew from the work of Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, notably their 1974 paper "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" in *Science*.

Biases are not a sign of stupidity. They come from mental shortcuts (heuristics) that are fast and usually good enough, but fail in predictable situations. Knowing them does not remove them, so the practical goal is to design checks into the process.

## Why it matters

Many costly mistakes in plans, investments and projects share the same few causes. Naming a bias gives a team a quick way to flag it, and a matching countermeasure gives a way to correct it.

## Common biases and countermeasures

| Bias | What happens | Countermeasure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmation bias | You look for and favour information that supports what you already believe | Seek disconfirming evidence on purpose; ask "what would prove me wrong?" |
| Anchoring | The first number or idea you see pulls later judgments towards it | Make your own estimate before seeing others; consider a wide range |
| Availability | Vivid or recent examples seem more common than they are | Check base rates and data, not memory or stories |
| Sunk cost fallacy | You continue because of what you have already spent | Judge only future costs and benefits |
| Overconfidence | You are surer than the evidence allows | Give ranges; track predictions against results |
| Planning fallacy | You underestimate time and cost, even with experience | Use past similar projects as a reference; add a buffer |
| Hindsight bias | After an outcome, it seems it was obvious all along | Write down predictions and reasons beforehand |
| Framing effect | The same facts lead to different choices depending on wording | Restate the choice in several ways |
| Loss aversion | Losses weigh more than equal gains | Ask what you would choose if starting from zero |
| Status quo bias | You prefer the current state because it is familiar | Ask what you would choose if it were new |
| Survivorship bias | You study the successes and ignore those that failed | Ask what happened to the cases you do not see |
| Groupthink | A cohesive group suppresses dissent in order to agree | Collect views in writing first; assign a challenger |
| Halo effect | One positive trait colours your view of the rest | Rate each criterion separately |
| Fundamental attribution error | You blame people's character and overlook the situation | Ask what in the situation could explain it |

The name "planning fallacy" comes from Kahneman and Tversky (1979), and "groupthink" from Irving Janis (1972). Some effects found in research have proved smaller or less consistent than first reported, so treat the list as a set of warnings, not laws.

## When to use

- Before a major decision, as a final check
- In a review of a forecast, estimate or plan
- When a decision was made quickly or emotionally
- During a post-mortem to understand why a call went wrong

## When not to use

- As a way to dismiss someone's view by labelling it "just a bias"
- For routine decisions; the checks cost more than they save
- As a substitute for evidence

## How to apply

1. **Identify the decision** and the moment where judgment mattered.
2. **Go through the list.** For each bias, ask whether it could be at work.
3. **Mark the likely ones.** Look for signs: a very early anchor, only supportive evidence, spending already made, a story that is too neat.
4. **Pick a countermeasure** for each likely bias from the table.
5. **Change the process,** not only your intention: add a step, a person or a rule.
6. **Record the decision and reasons,** so that you can check them later.
7. **Review the outcome.** Did a bias show up? Adjust the checklist.

## Debiasing habits

- Write predictions with probabilities, then check them
- Estimate before you see others' numbers
- Use a pre-mortem on important plans
- Get an outside view: how did similar cases turn out?
- Slow down when tired, rushed or emotional
- Decide in advance what evidence would change your mind

## Example: a project estimate

**Situation:** A team estimates a software project at 6 months.

| Check | Finding | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Planning fallacy | Their last three projects ran 40-60% over | Add a buffer based on the history, say 8-9 months |
| Anchoring | The 6-month figure came from the sponsor's hope | Re-estimate independently, bottom up |
| Overconfidence | Each task has a single-point estimate | Give a range for each |
| Groupthink | The team lead spoke first | Each member writes an estimate before discussion |

**Result:** The revised plan says 8 months with a range of 7-10, and the sponsor decides on a smaller first release.

## Pitfalls

- **Bias spotting as a weapon.** Using the terms to win arguments helps nobody.
- **Believing you are immune.** Awareness alone does little.
- **Checking only others.** Apply the list to your own decisions first.
- **Overcorrecting.** Doubting everything is paralysing.
- **No change to the process.** Without a new step, nothing changes.
- **Treating the list as complete.** It is a starting set.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Decision and moment identified
- [ ] List reviewed for likely biases
- [ ] A countermeasure chosen for each
- [ ] Process changed (step, person or rule)
- [ ] Decision and reasons recorded
- [ ] Outcome reviewed later
