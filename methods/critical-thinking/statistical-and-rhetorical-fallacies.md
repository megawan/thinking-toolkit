# Statistical and Rhetorical Fallacies

> A second catalogue of reasoning errors: those that misuse numbers and probability, those that win arguments by tricks, and the formal mistakes of logic.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 10-30 minutes to review an argument or a set of figures |
| **Best for** | Checking claims that rely on statistics, probability or persuasive technique, as a companion to the main fallacy guide |

## What it is

The main guide, [Logical Fallacies](logical-fallacies.md), covers the most common informal fallacies. This guide adds three groups it leaves out:

1. **Statistical and probability fallacies**: mistakes in reading numbers, chance and samples
2. **Rhetorical fallacies**: techniques that win an argument without answering it
3. **Formal fallacies**: invalid argument forms, whatever the content

No list can be complete, and the names matter less than the pattern. Use the catalogue to ask better questions.

## When to use

- A report, article or pitch leans on statistics, probability or a persuasive technique
- You are about to quote a figure and want to check it
- An argument avoids the question or changes the terms of the debate
- You are teaching or practising careful reasoning

## When not to use

- As a way to dismiss someone by naming a fallacy without explaining it
- To reject a conclusion just because the argument for it is flawed
- When the real need is more data, not a critique of the reasoning

## 1. Statistical and probability fallacies

| Fallacy | What goes wrong | Example | A better question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gambler's fallacy | Believing past independent outcomes change the next one | "Red has not come up for ten spins, so red is due." | "Are these events really independent?" |
| Base rate neglect | Ignoring how common something is to begin with | "The test is 90% accurate, so a positive means I probably have it." | "How common is it in people like me?" See [Bayesian Thinking](../mental-models/bayesian-thinking.md). |
| Prosecutor's fallacy | Confusing the chance of the evidence given innocence with the chance of innocence given the evidence | "The chance of this match by accident is one in a million, so he is almost certainly guilty." | "How likely are the competing explanations?" |
| Regression to the mean | Crediting a treatment for a return to normal after an extreme result | "Sales were terrible, we changed the manager, and sales improved." | "Would it have improved anyway?" |
| Simpson's paradox | A trend that appears in each group reverses when the groups are combined | A treatment works better in each hospital but worse overall, because the hospitals treat different mixes of patients | "Is the combined figure hiding differences between groups?" |
| Texas sharpshooter | Looking at the data first, then drawing the target around a cluster | "Look, these five towns have high rates of the illness!" | "Was the pattern predicted before looking, or found after?" |
| Anecdotal fallacy | Using a personal story as proof of a general rule | "My uncle smoked and lived to 95." | "What does the data on many cases show?" |
| Ecological fallacy | Inferring facts about individuals from group averages | "This region has high income, so each resident is rich." | "Does the group average apply to individuals?" |
| Small sample | Reading a pattern into too few cases | "Three customers complained, so the product is failing." | "How many cases, and how were they chosen?" |
| False precision | Reporting a figure with more accuracy than the data allow | "Our market is $4,382,917." | "How uncertain is this figure?" |

## 2. Rhetorical fallacies

| Fallacy | What it does | Example | A fair response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Loaded question | Packs an unproven assumption into a question | "Have you stopped cutting corners on safety?" | "That question assumes something I reject." |
| Poisoning the well | Discredits a source in advance | "Don't listen to her; she works for the vendor." | "Judge what she says on its merits." |
| Special pleading | Applies a rule to others but claims an exception for oneself, without a reason | "Deadlines matter, except for my project." | "What justifies the exception?" |
| Moving the goalposts | Changes the standard after it has been met | "You fixed that, but now it must also be cheaper." | "We agreed the criterion earlier; has it changed?" |
| Whataboutism | Answers a criticism by pointing to someone else's fault | "What about the other team's errors?" | "That may be a separate problem; let's finish this one." |
| False equivalence | Treats two very different things as the same | "Both sides make mistakes, so they are equally at fault." | "How do they compare in size and kind?" |
| Middle ground | Assumes the truth lies halfway between two positions | "One says 0, the other says 100, so it must be 50." | "Why would the midpoint be right?" |
| Nirvana fallacy | Rejects a workable solution because it is not perfect | "A lock won't stop every burglar, so why bother?" | "Is it better than the alternative?" |
| Appeal to tradition | Treats something as good because it has long been done | "We've always done it this way." | "Is it good, or only familiar?" |
| Appeal to novelty | Treats something as good because it is new | "It's the latest version, so it must be better." | "Better on what measure?" |
| Appeal to pity | Uses sympathy in place of reasons | "I worked all weekend on this; you can't reject it." | "I appreciate the effort; does the work meet the need?" |
| Fallacy of composition | Assumes what is true of the parts is true of the whole | "Each part is lightweight, so the whole machine is lightweight." | "Does the whole behave like its parts?" |
| Fallacy of division | Assumes what is true of the whole is true of each part | "The firm is successful, so every department is." | "Does the whole's property apply to every part?" |
| Ad hoc rescue | Adds an excuse to save a failing claim | "The forecast was right; the market just did not follow it." | "What result would count against it?" See [Falsifiability](falsifiability.md). |

## 3. Formal fallacies

A formal fallacy is an invalid argument form: the conclusion does not follow, whatever the content. Compare the valid forms with their invalid look-alikes.

| Form | Pattern | Example | Valid? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Modus ponens | If P then Q. P. So Q. | If it rains, the street is wet. It rains. So the street is wet. | Valid |
| Modus tollens | If P then Q. Not Q. So not P. | If it rains, the street is wet. The street is dry. So it did not rain. | Valid |
| Affirming the consequent | If P then Q. Q. So P. | If it rains, the street is wet. The street is wet. So it rained. | Invalid: a pipe may have burst |
| Denying the antecedent | If P then Q. Not P. So not Q. | If it rains, the street is wet. It did not rain. So the street is dry. | Invalid: a street cleaner may have been past |
| Undistributed middle | All A are C. All B are C. So all A are B. | All dogs are animals. All cats are animals. So all dogs are cats. | Invalid |
| Affirming a disjunct | P or Q. P. So not Q. | Ann or Ben is in the office. Ann is. So Ben is not. | Invalid if "or" allows both |

## How to apply

1. **Find the claim and its support.** Is it based on numbers, a persuasive move, or a chain of "if... then"?
2. **For numbers, ask four things:** compared with what (base rate, comparison group), how many cases, how were they chosen, and was the pattern predicted or found after looking.
3. **For independence, ask:** do earlier outcomes really affect later ones? Coin flips do not; weather, sales and family risks may.
4. **For tricks, restate the move in plain words:** "You are changing the standard", "That question assumes...". Do not only name the fallacy.
5. **For logic, write the form:** If P then Q, and so on. Check it against the valid forms.
6. **Ask for the missing information:** the comparison, the sample, the criterion, the exception.
7. **Keep the conclusion open.** An argument may be flawed and the conclusion still true (see the fallacy fallacy in [Logical Fallacies](logical-fallacies.md)).
8. **Check your own reasoning** against the same list, especially for numbers you are about to quote.

## Example (hypothetical): a hospital league table

**Claim in a report:** "Hospital A has a higher death rate than Hospital B, so it provides worse care."

| Check | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| Base rate and groups | Hospital A treats more severely ill patients |
| Combined versus split | Within each severity group, A's rate is no worse than B's (Simpson's paradox) |
| Sample | Some groups are very small |

**Response:** "Please show the rates by patient severity, and the number of cases in each group, before we compare the two."

## Pitfalls

- **Name-dropping fallacies.** Explain the flaw in plain words.
- **Rejecting a conclusion because of a flaw.** It can still be true.
- **Applying statistical rules without context.** Streaks can be evidence of a biased process; the gambler's fallacy applies when events are truly independent.
- **Overlooking your own numbers.** Check base rates and samples in your own reports.
- **Treating a rhetorical move as always wrong.** Pointing out an exception can be fair if the reason is stated.
- **Over-reading small differences.** A small gap with a small sample may be chance.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Type of claim identified: numbers, persuasion or logic
- [ ] Base rates, sample size and comparison checked
- [ ] Independence of events questioned
- [ ] Rhetorical move restated in plain words
- [ ] Logical form checked
- [ ] Missing information requested
- [ ] Conclusion left open
- [ ] Own reasoning checked
