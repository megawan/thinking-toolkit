# Correlation vs Causation

> Two things moving together does not mean one causes the other; check for other explanations before you act.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 15-45 minutes |
| **Best for** | Reading statistics, evaluating studies, and deciding whether a change really caused a result |

## What it is

Two variables are **correlated** if they tend to move together. One **causes** the other only if changing the first changes the second. Correlation is easy to observe; causation is harder to show.

When two things are correlated, there are several possible explanations.

| Explanation | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A causes B | The straightforward reading | Smoking raises the risk of lung cancer |
| B causes A | Reverse causation | Does stress cause poor sleep, or does poor sleep cause stress? |
| C causes both | A confounding (third) variable | Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in hot weather |
| Chance | Coincidence, especially with many comparisons | Two unrelated curves that happen to match over a few years |
| Selection | The groups differ in some other way | People who take a supplement may also exercise more |
| Both influence each other | A feedback loop | More practice improves skill, and better skill encourages practice |

## Why it matters

Mistaking correlation for causation leads to wasted effort and harm: investing in a change that does nothing, or missing the real cause. It is behind many errors in business, health and policy.

## When to use

- A report claims that X leads to Y
- You are deciding whether to roll out a change after a trend
- Reading studies and news about health, education or the economy
- Designing a test to find out what really works

## When not to use

- You only need a prediction and not an explanation; correlation can be enough to forecast
- Causes are already established by strong evidence
- Experiments are impossible and the stakes call for a decision on the best available evidence

## How to apply

1. **State the claim:** "X causes Y."
2. **Check the timing.** Did X come before Y?
3. **Consider the reverse.** Could Y cause X?
4. **Look for a third factor.** What could affect both?
5. **Consider chance.** How many comparisons were made? How large is the sample?
6. **Check who is compared.** Are the groups alike apart from X? Is there selection?
7. **Look for a comparison group** ("control"). What happened to similar people or places without X?
8. **Prefer experiments.** A randomised controlled trial gives a fair comparison, because chance decides who gets X.
9. **Weigh the wider evidence.** Is there a plausible mechanism, a dose-response pattern, consistency across studies, and no better explanation?
10. **State your conclusion carefully:** "associated with", "likely causes", or "shown to cause", as the evidence allows.

## Bradford Hill's viewpoints

In 1965, the epidemiologist Austin Bradford Hill described considerations that help judge whether an association is causal.

| Viewpoint | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Strength | Is the association large? |
| Consistency | Do different studies find it? |
| Specificity | Is the effect linked to this cause in particular? |
| Temporality | Does the cause come before the effect? |
| Biological gradient | More exposure, more effect? |
| Plausibility and coherence | Is there a believable mechanism, and does it fit what is already known? |
| Experiment | Does removing the cause reduce the effect? |
| Analogy | Is there a similar established case? |

These are aids to judgement, not a checklist that proves causation.

## Example: hormone therapy and heart disease

Before the Women's Health Initiative (WHI), the belief that hormone therapy (HT) protected the heart after menopause rested largely on observational studies. When the WHI designed randomised, placebo-controlled trials, the hypothesis was that HT would reduce heart disease.

In July 2002 the estrogen-plus-progestin arm was stopped after an average follow-up of 5.2 years, because it showed increased risks of breast cancer, heart disease, stroke and pulmonary embolism. The estrogen-alone arm was stopped in February 2004, with no apparent effect on heart disease and an increased stroke risk. The trial results differed from what the observational studies had suggested.

**Lesson:** Observed association was not enough. Only a controlled comparison showed what HT did, and the answer was different.

## Everyday examples

| Observation | Possible explanations |
| --- | --- |
| Teams with more meetings have higher output | Busy, high-output teams need more coordination (reverse); a large project drives both (third factor) |
| Customers who use a feature stay longer | Engaged customers use more features (selection); the feature may or may not keep them |
| Sales rose after the new advert | Season, price, competitors, or chance |

## Pitfalls

- **Assuming A causes B because A came first.** This is the post hoc error.
- **Ignoring confounders.** Ask what else differs between the groups.
- **Cherry-picking correlations.** Test enough pairs and some will match by chance.
- **Reading too much into one study.** Look for replication.
- **Equating "not significant" with "no effect".** A small study may miss a real effect.
- **Ignoring practical significance.** A tiny effect can be statistically clear and still unimportant.
- **Over-correcting.** Some correlations do reflect causes; the aim is to test, not to dismiss.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Claim stated as a cause
- [ ] Timing and reverse causation checked
- [ ] Possible third factors listed
- [ ] Chance and sample size considered
- [ ] Comparison group examined
- [ ] Experiment or strong wider evidence sought
- [ ] Conclusion worded to match the evidence
