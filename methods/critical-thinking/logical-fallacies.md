# Logical Fallacies

> Learn the common ways an argument can look convincing while its reasoning fails, so you can spot them and answer them fairly.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 10-30 minutes to review an argument; the catalogue is a reference |
| **Best for** | Evaluating arguments in meetings, articles, adverts and debates, and checking your own reasoning |

## What it is

A fallacy is faulty reasoning in an argument. It can look well-reasoned if nobody notices the flaw. The study of fallacies goes back to Aristotle, whose *Sophistical Refutations* is usually cited as the first systematic treatment.

Fallacies are commonly split into two kinds.

| Kind | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formal | The structure of a deductive argument is invalid, whatever the content | "If it rained, the street is wet. The street is wet. So it rained." (affirming the consequent; the street could be wet for another reason) |
| Informal | The error lies in content, relevance or context, even if the form looks valid | Attacking the speaker instead of the point |

A fallacy does not always make the conclusion false. The conclusion may be true by accident; the problem is that the argument does not show it. Fallacies arise on purpose (to persuade or manipulate) and by accident (carelessness, bias, ignorance or loose language). They differ from [Cognitive Biases](cognitive-biases.md), which are patterns in how people think, not flaws in how an argument is built.

## Why it matters

Arguments shape decisions at work and in public life. A fallacy can hide a weak case behind a confident tone. Knowing the common patterns lets you ask for the missing support, and helps you avoid the same errors in your own writing.

## When to use

- Reading a proposal, report or news story
- Preparing or reviewing your own argument
- Meetings where a decision rests on a persuasive but thin case
- Teaching others to question claims

## When not to use

- As a way to win by name-calling ("that's a fallacy!") without explaining the flaw
- To dismiss a conclusion just because the argument for it was flawed (see the fallacy fallacy below)
- When the real issue is a lack of evidence; ask for evidence directly

## Common informal fallacies

| Fallacy | What it does | Example | A fair response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ad hominem | Attacks the person instead of the argument | "Her budget can't be right; she failed accounting at school." | "Let's check the figures themselves." |
| Straw man | Misrepresents a position, then refutes the distortion | "She wants to trim travel costs, so she wants us to stop visiting customers." | "That is not what I said. My proposal is..." |
| False dilemma | Presents two options when more exist | "Either we cut prices or we lose every customer." | "What other options are there?" |
| Slippery slope | Claims a small step will inevitably lead to disaster, without support | "If we allow one late submission, nobody will meet a deadline again." | "What makes each step likely?" |
| Appeal to authority | Treats a claim as true because an authority said it, outside their field or without evidence | "A famous actor says this supplement works." | "What is the evidence, and what is their expertise?" |
| Appeal to popularity | Treats a claim as true because many believe or do it | "Everyone is switching, so it must be better." | "Does popularity show quality here?" |
| Appeal to nature | Assumes natural means good or unnatural means bad | "It's natural, so it's safe." | "Natural things can be harmful; what does the evidence say?" |
| Appeal to emotion | Uses feelings in place of reasons | A photo of a sad child to sell an unrelated product | "I understand the feeling; what is the argument?" |
| Post hoc | Assumes that because B followed A, A caused B | "Sales rose after we changed the logo, so the logo worked." | "What else changed? Is there a comparison?" See [Correlation vs Causation](correlation-vs-causation.md). |
| Hasty generalisation | Draws a broad conclusion from too few cases | "I met two rude drivers there; the people are rude." | "How large and representative is the sample?" |
| Circular reasoning | The conclusion is assumed in the premise | "The report is reliable because it says it is reliable." | "What independent support is there?" |
| Red herring | Introduces an irrelevant point to change the subject | "Why talk about the delays when we won an award last year?" | "That's good, but let's return to the delays." |
| Tu quoque | Dismisses a criticism because the critic does the same | "You tell me to save, but you overspend." | "That may be so, but is the advice sound?" |
| Equivocation | Shifts the meaning of a word within an argument | "Laws should be obeyed; gravity is a law; so gravity must be obeyed." | "The word means different things here." |
| No true Scotsman | Redefines a group to exclude a counter-example | "No real engineer would make that error." | "Is that a fact about engineers or a definition?" |
| Genetic fallacy | Judges a claim by its origin, not its merit | "That idea came from a competitor, so it's bad." | "Judge the idea on its merits." |
| Argument from ignorance | Claims something is true because it hasn't been disproved (or false because not proved) | "No one has shown it's unsafe, so it's safe." | "Absence of evidence is not evidence either way unless we looked properly." See [Burden of Proof](burden-of-proof.md). |
| Cherry-picking | Selects only the evidence that fits | Quoting the one month when sales rose | "What does the full period show?" |

## The fallacy fallacy

Showing that an argument contains a fallacy does not show that its conclusion is false. The conclusion may be true for other reasons. Say "that argument does not support the claim", not "so you are wrong".

## How to apply

1. **Restate the argument.** Identify the conclusion and the premises offered for it. If you cannot, ask the speaker.
2. **Check relevance.** Do the premises bear on the conclusion?
3. **Check support.** Is each premise true, or backed by evidence? How strong is the link to the conclusion?
4. **Look for gaps.** Is something assumed but not stated? A [Toulmin model](argument-mapping-and-toulmin.md) can help.
5. **Ask if a pattern fits.** If a fallacy applies, name the flaw in plain words, not only the label.
6. **Respond fairly.** Restate the best version of their argument ([Steel-manning](steel-manning.md)), point out the gap, and ask for what is missing.
7. **Keep the conclusion open.** Ask whether other support might exist.
8. **Check your own arguments** against the same list before you present them.

## Example: a meeting about a software tool

**Claim in the meeting:** "We should keep the old reporting tool. The vendor of the new one is a start-up with only 10 staff, and everyone knows start-ups fail."

| Part | Analysis |
| --- | --- |
| Hasty generalisation | "Start-ups fail" is about many companies; this one may be sound |
| Red herring | Vendor size is not the same as tool quality for the team's needs |
| Missing support | No data on the old tool's cost, the new tool's features or the vendor's finances |

**Response:** "Vendor risk is a fair concern. Let's check this vendor's finances and our exit options, and compare both tools on the features we need."

## Pitfalls

- **Labelling instead of explaining.** Say what is wrong, not only the name.
- **The fallacy fallacy.** A flawed argument can still have a true conclusion.
- **Seeing fallacies only in others.** Check your own reasoning.
- **Treating every appeal as a fallacy.** An expert's view is evidence; the problem is treating it as proof.
- **Ignoring context.** Whether an argument is fallacious often depends on content and context. A similar argument may be sound in another setting.
- **Uncharitable reading.** Restate the argument fairly before you criticise it.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Conclusion and premises identified
- [ ] Relevance and support checked
- [ ] Hidden assumptions noted
- [ ] Flaw explained in plain words
- [ ] Response fair, with a request for what is missing
- [ ] Conclusion not dismissed only because of the fallacy
- [ ] Own argument checked too
