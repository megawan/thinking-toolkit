# Steel-manning

> State the other side's argument in its strongest, fairest form before you respond to it.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 10-30 minutes |
| **Best for** | Disagreements, debates, reviews of proposals, and testing your own position |

## What it is

Steel-manning is the opposite of straw-manning. A **straw man** is a weak or distorted version of an opponent's argument that is easy to knock down. A **steel man** is the best version of that argument, one its supporters would accept as fair, and perhaps even better than they put it themselves.

A well-known way to do this comes from the social psychologist Anatol Rapoport and was popularised by the philosopher Daniel Dennett in his book *Intuition Pumps* (2013). The rules are:

1. Re-express the other person's position so clearly, vividly and fairly that they say, "Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way."
2. List any points of agreement, especially ones that are not general or widely shared.
3. Mention anything you have learned from them.
4. Only then say anything by way of rebuttal or criticism.

## Why it works

We tend to argue against the weakest version of an opposing view, which makes us feel right but teaches us nothing. Building the strongest version forces you to understand the reasons behind it. Either you find a flaw that survives the strong version, and your criticism is stronger, or you find that the view has merit, and you update. The other person also feels heard, which makes agreement or a respectful disagreement more likely.

## When to use

- You disagree with someone and want to resolve it
- Reviewing a proposal you are inclined to reject
- Before a decision with strong views on both sides
- Testing your own view for weaknesses

## When not to use

- The other side is arguing in bad faith, or the question is a matter of established fact
- You need an urgent decision and have no time to discuss
- The "view" is a harmful demand and not an argument

## How to apply

1. **Listen first.** Ask questions until you understand what they believe and why.
2. **Identify the core claim,** the evidence and the values behind it.
3. **Restate it** in your own words, as strongly as you can. Include the best evidence and the most reasonable motives.
4. **Check with them.** Ask: "Have I got it right? What am I missing?" Adjust until they agree.
5. **List what you agree with,** and what you have learned.
6. **Find the real disagreement.** Is it about facts, values or priorities?
7. **Respond to the strong version.** Address the best form of the argument, not a weaker one.
8. **Be willing to change your mind** or to refine your position.

## Prompt questions

- What is the strongest reason someone might hold this view?
- What evidence would support it?
- What values lie behind it?
- Under what conditions would it be right?
- What would convince me that I am wrong?

## Example 1: a proposal to switch to a four-day week

**Your view:** Opposed, because of customer coverage.

**Straw man:** "They want everyone to work less and be paid the same."

**Steel man:** "The supporters believe that focused time and rest raise output and cut burnout, that the company can keep coverage with rotating cover, and that a trial would allow the company to measure the results. They point to lower turnover and easier hiring, which carry real costs."

**What changes:** Instead of rejecting the idea, you propose a trial with defined coverage and success measures, which answers your concern and respects theirs.

## Example 2: a code review dispute

**Reviewer:** "This abstraction is over-engineered."

**Steel man of the author's view:** "The author expects three more payment providers within a year, and a simple version would require a rewrite each time. The extra layer costs a day now and saves a week each time."

**Result:** The reviewer asks whether the three providers are firm. If they are, the abstraction stays. If not, they agree to defer it.

## Pitfalls

- **Patronising restatement.** If they would not recognise it as their view, it is not a steel man.
- **Steel-manning only to dismiss.** The aim is to understand.
- **Weak steel mans of views you dislike.** Check that you have given them the same care.
- **Skipping the agreement step.** It builds trust.
- **Treating all views as equally valid.** Fair does not mean correct.
- **Doing it once.** Check your understanding again as the discussion moves.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Listened and asked questions
- [ ] Restated their view in its strongest form
- [ ] They confirmed the restatement
- [ ] Agreements and lessons listed
- [ ] The real disagreement identified
- [ ] Response addresses the strong version
