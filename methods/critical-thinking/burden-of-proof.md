# Burden of Proof

> Whoever makes a claim has to support it; asking others to disprove it is not an answer.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Critical thinking |
| **Time needed** | 5-20 minutes |
| **Best for** | Debates, proposals and claims where it is unclear who needs to supply the evidence |

## What it is

The burden of proof is the obligation to provide enough evidence to support a claim. The traditional Latin phrase is *onus probandi*. As a rule, the person who makes a claim, especially a positive or surprising one, carries the burden. Others are not obliged to disprove it.

Bertrand Russell made the point with his teapot analogy. He wrote that if he claimed a china teapot orbits the Sun between Earth and Mars, too small to detect with any telescope, nobody could disprove it, but it would still be nonsense to say that doubting it is unreasonable. The burden of proof lies with the person who makes the claim.

## Standards of proof

How much evidence is needed depends on the setting.

| Setting | Standard | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Criminal law | Beyond reasonable doubt | Very high; protects against wrongful conviction |
| Civil law | Balance of probabilities (preponderance of evidence) | More likely than not |
| Science | Strong, repeatable evidence, often with statistical thresholds | Claims must withstand replication |
| Everyday decisions | Proportionate to the stakes | The riskier the claim, the more support |

A related rule of thumb, popularised by Carl Sagan, says that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence: the more a claim conflicts with what is well established, the stronger the support it needs.

## Why it works

Without the principle, any claim could be made and left standing until someone proved it false, which is often impossible. Putting the burden on the claimant keeps discussion tied to evidence, protects people from baseless accusations and stops discussions from being won by the person who makes the boldest statement.

## When to use

- A claim is made without support
- A debate is stuck because each side demands that the other prove them wrong
- Setting up a decision, a trial or a test
- Deciding how much evidence you need before you act

## When not to use

- As a shield to avoid ever supporting your own position; once you make a claim, you also carry a burden
- When the burden has been clearly shifted by agreement or law (for example, a person who relies on an exception)
- As a rule for every doubt; sometimes the cost of being wrong is high enough to justify precaution without proof

## How to apply

1. **Identify the claim.** What exactly is being asserted, and by whom?
2. **Ask who is making the positive claim.** Usually that person carries the burden.
3. **Check the setting.** What standard of proof applies: legal, scientific, or practical?
4. **Scale the evidence to the claim.** Surprising or costly claims need more.
5. **Ask for the support.** "What is the evidence?" "How was it collected?"
6. **Judge the support.** Is it relevant, sufficient and from a reliable source? See [Argument Mapping and Toulmin Model](argument-mapping-and-toulmin.md).
7. **Notice shifting.** Watch for "prove me wrong" and "you can't show it isn't so", which turn the burden around.
8. **Decide what to do if the burden is not met.** Reserve judgment, apply a precaution, or reject the claim.
9. **Remember your own burden** when you assert something.

## Example 1: a product claim

**Claim from a supplier:** "Our software will cut your processing time by half."

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Who has the burden? | The supplier, who made the claim |
| What standard? | Proportionate to the cost: a pilot or reference data |
| What counts as support? | A trial on the buyer's own data, or independent customer results |

**Result:** The buyer asks for a two-week trial. The supplier's claim stays unproved until then.

## Example 2: burden shifting in a meeting

**Colleague:** "The new process is a disaster. Prove it isn't."

**Response:** "Please show me where it has failed. What data do you have? If you can share it, we can look at it together."

The colleague made the claim, so they need to supply the grounds first.

## A note on negative claims

Some argue that only positive claims need proof. Critics reply that every truth claim, positive or negative, has a burden of proof. In practice, a claim such as "this drug has no side effects" also needs evidence, since it asserts something definite. The better rule is that anyone who asserts something specific should be ready to support it, and that more surprising or higher-stakes assertions need more support.

## Pitfalls

- **Prove me wrong.** Asking others to disprove a claim moves the burden unfairly.
- **Using absence of evidence as proof.** "No one has shown it's unsafe" does not show it is safe unless a proper search was made.
- **Demanding impossible proof.** Be clear about the standard you want and why.
- **Applying the principle selectively.** Hold your own views to the same standard.
- **Ignoring precaution.** When harm could be severe, acting on less than full proof may be reasonable.
- **Treating the teapot as the answer to every question.** Context and evidence still matter.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Claim and claimant identified
- [ ] Standard of proof agreed
- [ ] Evidence scaled to the claim
- [ ] Support examined for relevance and reliability
- [ ] Burden shifting noticed
- [ ] Decision made if the burden is not met
- [ ] Own claims supported
