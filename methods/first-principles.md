# First Principles

> Break a problem down to what is certainly true, then build the solution up from there instead of copying what already exists.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Problem solving |
| **Time needed** | 30-60 minutes, solo or with a small group |
| **Best for** | Questioning "how it has always been done", redesigning costs, products, or processes |

## What it is

First principles thinking means reducing a problem to its most basic elements, the facts that cannot be reduced further, and reasoning upward from them. The idea goes back to Aristotle, who defined a first principle as "the first basis from which a thing is known".

The usual alternative is reasoning by analogy: "others do it this way, so we will too". Analogy is fast and often good enough, but it carries along every assumption and inefficiency of what it copies.

## Why it works

Most conventions exist for historical reasons that may no longer apply. By separating what is actually true from what is merely inherited, you can see options that were hidden by habit. It is especially useful when the existing answers are too expensive, too slow, or clearly stuck.

## When to use

- The standard solution costs too much or performs poorly
- You are told "that is just how it works" and cannot see why
- You are designing something new with no good precedent
- A market, process, or product feels full of unexamined assumptions

## When not to use

- A proven solution fits well and the stakes are low
- Time is short and the decision is easily reversed
- The problem is routine; the effort will outweigh the gain

## How to apply

1. **Define the problem or goal.** Be specific about what you want to achieve.
2. **List current assumptions.** Write down everything you believe about how the problem works or must be solved.
3. **Challenge each assumption.** Ask: How do I know this is true? What is the evidence? Is it a law of nature, a rule, or only a habit?
4. **Sort into two groups.** Hard facts (physics, math, legal requirements, measured data) and assumptions or conventions.
5. **Reduce to fundamentals.** Keep asking "what is this made of?" or "why is this required?" until you reach hard facts.
6. **Rebuild.** Starting only from the facts, design a solution without reference to existing ones.
7. **Compare and test.** Check the new design against the current one and test the riskiest part cheaply.

## Useful questions

- What do we know for certain, and how do we know it?
- What is this made of, at the most basic level?
- If we started from scratch today, what would we do?
- Which constraint is real, and which is inherited?
- What would have to be true for this to be possible?

## Example 1: Cost of a product

**Problem:** Custom shelving costs $400 and customers think it is too expensive.

- *By analogy:* Competitors charge $350-450, so we adjust prices a little.
- *First principles:* What is a shelf made of? Wood, hardware, labour, delivery, retail margin. Raw material is $60. Labour is 2 hours. Most of the rest is delivery of a bulky finished item and a store margin.
- *Assumptions challenged:* It must be sold assembled. It must be sold in a store.
- *Rebuild:* Sell flat-pack cut-to-size panels directly online, with simple assembly.

**Result:** Lower shipping volume, no store margin, and a price near $180.

## Example 2: Personal productivity

**Problem:** I never finish my weekly report on time.

- *Assumptions:* It must be written on Friday. It must include every project. It must be formatted like last year's.
- *Facts:* My manager needs three things by Monday: status, blockers, next steps.
- *Rebuild:* Keep a running note during the week, and send a half-page summary of those three items on Friday.

## Pitfalls

- **Stopping too early.** If an "assumption" is still a belief, keep digging.
- **Treating every convention as wrong.** Many conventions exist for good reasons; verify before discarding.
- **Using it everywhere.** It is slow. Reserve it for problems that justify the effort.
- **Confusing fundamentals with preferences.** A strong opinion is not a fact.
- **Ignoring practical limits.** A design from scratch still has to be buildable and affordable.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Goal stated clearly
- [ ] Assumptions written out
- [ ] Each assumption checked against evidence
- [ ] Facts separated from conventions
- [ ] New solution built from facts only
- [ ] Riskiest part tested
