# Lateral Thinking

> Step sideways instead of pushing harder down the same road, to find solutions logic alone would not reach.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Idea generation |
| **Time needed** | 20-60 minutes, solo or in a group |
| **Best for** | Problems where the obvious solutions have failed or feel stale |

## What it is

Lateral thinking is a set of deliberate techniques for generating ideas from unexpected directions. The term was coined by Edward de Bono in 1967.

Normal (vertical) thinking moves step by step, each step following logically from the last, and you dig deeper into the same hole. Lateral thinking asks you to leave the hole and dig somewhere else. It does not replace logical analysis. The two work together: lateral thinking produces options, vertical thinking evaluates and develops them.

## Why it works

The mind builds patterns from experience and then applies them automatically. This is efficient, but it means we keep reaching for familiar answers and rarely question how a problem is framed. Lateral thinking techniques interrupt these patterns on purpose, so that ideas that never appear in a normal chain of reasoning get a chance.

## When to use

- You are stuck and every option looks like a variation of the same idea
- Conventional solutions have already been tried and failed
- You want many candidate ideas before picking one
- The problem itself may be framed in a limiting way
- You are designing something new rather than fixing something broken

## When not to use

- The cause is unknown (find it first, for example with [Five Whys](../root-cause/five-whys.md))
- A proven, standard solution exists and works
- You must decide immediately, with no room to explore

## How to apply

1. **State the problem** in one clear sentence.
2. **Choose a technique** from the list below.
3. **Generate without judging.** Write down every idea, including silly or impossible ones. Aim for quantity.
4. **Switch technique** and repeat. Different techniques open different doors.
5. **Look for the useful core.** For each odd idea, ask what is interesting or workable in it.
6. **Evaluate.** Only now apply logic: feasibility, cost, risk, fit.
7. **Pick a few to test.** Prototype or try cheaply before committing.

## The techniques

### 1. Challenge assumptions

Every problem rests on beliefs that are rarely questioned.

**How:**

1. List every assumption you can find about the problem ("customers must register", "the report must be weekly", "this needs a meeting").
2. For each, ask: what if this were not true?
3. Explore what becomes possible.

**Example:** "A restaurant must have a menu printed on paper." If false: QR codes, a menu on the wall, a server who recites the day's dishes, no menu at all with a single set meal.

### 2. Random input

Use an unrelated word, object, or image to force new connections.

**How:**

1. Pick a random word (open a book, use a word list, or pick the first object you see).
2. List its properties, uses, and associations.
3. Force a link between each one and your problem.
4. Keep what sparks something real.

**Example:** Problem: employees ignore the internal newsletter. Random word: *lighthouse*. Associations: warns of danger, tall and visible, flashes in a pattern, guides ships. Ideas: a very short, regular "warning light" alert for only the things that affect people's work; put key news on a tall visible screen in the lobby; guide new hires with a "route" of what to read first.

### 3. Provocation (PO)

A provocation is a deliberately unreasonable statement, marked "PO", used as a stepping stone rather than a claim.

**How:**

1. Create a provocation by one of these methods:
   - **Reversal:** state the opposite ("PO: customers pay before they know the price")
   - **Exaggeration:** push a number to the extreme ("PO: the shop has ten thousand staff")
   - **Distortion:** change the order or relationship ("PO: we ship before the customer orders")
   - **Wishful thinking:** state the ideal ("PO: the product fixes itself")
2. Ask: what would be good about this? Under what conditions would it work?
3. Move from the provocation to a practical idea.

**Example:** "PO: we ship before the customer orders." Leads to: predictive stocking near likely buyers, subscriptions with auto-replenishment, pre-positioned inventory in the busiest regions.

### 4. Reversal

Turn the problem upside down.

**How:**

1. Restate the goal as its opposite ("How can we lose all our customers?").
2. List every way to achieve the opposite.
3. Invert each item into a possible solution.

**Example:** Goal: make onboarding faster. Reversed: how to make onboarding as slow as possible? Many forms, manual approval, unclear steps, no progress indicator. Inverted: fewer fields, automatic approval for low-risk cases, a visible checklist.

### 5. Alternatives

Resist the first workable answer.

**How:**

1. Set a quota before you begin (for example, 10 distinct approaches).
2. Keep going past the point where ideas feel exhausted.
3. Label each by its underlying approach and make sure they differ in kind, not just in detail.

**Example:** Problem: reduce late deliveries. Alternatives: faster couriers; earlier cutoff times; promise later dates; ship from closer warehouses; let customers pick up; charge for guaranteed dates; reduce what is sold per order.

### 6. Reframing the problem

Change how the problem is described and a different set of solutions appears.

**How:**

1. Restate the problem at a higher level ("why do we want this?") and a lower level ("what is a smaller piece?").
2. Restate it from another person's point of view (customer, competitor, newcomer).
3. Solve each version.

**Example:** "We need more parking" becomes "people need to get to work" (carpool, shuttle, remote days) or "cars sit idle all day" (shared use, valet stacking).

## Example: abandoned checkout

**Situation:** A small online store loses many customers at the checkout page.

| Technique | Output | Useful core |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Challenge assumptions | "Customers must create an account." | Offer guest checkout |
| Reversal | How to make people abandon checkout: hide shipping costs, add fields, slow the page. | Show total cost early, cut fields, speed up the page |
| Random input: *umbrella* | Protects you from something unexpected. | Show a return guarantee at the payment step |
| Provocation: "PO: the customer pays after receiving the product" | Pay on delivery or trial period. | Offer pay-on-delivery for first orders |

**Result:** Four testable ideas. After evaluation, guest checkout and early cost display are cheapest to try first.

## Working in a group

- Give people a few minutes to write ideas alone before sharing, so loud voices do not set the direction.
- Ban criticism during generation. Collect first, evaluate later.
- Use one technique at a time and timebox it (5-10 minutes).
- Ask each person to build on someone else's idea before presenting their own.

## Pitfalls

- **Judging too early.** Evaluation kills half-formed ideas that could grow.
- **Treating a provocation as the answer.** It is a stepping stone.
- **Stopping at the first good idea.** Quantity leads to quality.
- **Skipping evaluation.** Not every idea is practical, and the method needs a logical filter at the end.
- **Using it on the wrong problem.** If the cause is unclear, diagnose first.
- **Forcing it.** If one technique is not producing results, switch to another.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Problem stated in one sentence
- [ ] At least two different techniques used
- [ ] Ideas written down without judging
- [ ] Each odd idea examined for a useful core
- [ ] A shortlist selected and a cheap test planned
