# Analogical Thinking

> Borrow a solution from a different field by asking what your problem is like.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Idea generation |
| **Time needed** | 30-60 minutes, solo or in a group |
| **Best for** | Problems that are stuck inside one field's usual answers |

## What it is

Analogical thinking means solving a problem by finding something similar in another domain and adapting its solution. Instead of asking "how do people in my field solve this?", you ask "where else does this same problem exist, and how is it solved there?"

The method is central to **synectics**, developed by William J. J. Gordon in the 1950s and 1960s, which uses several kinds of analogy to make the unfamiliar familiar and the familiar strange.

A well-known example is Velcro. Georges de Mestral noticed how burrs clung to his dog's fur and examined them under a microscope. The tiny hooks gave him the design for a fastener. Engineering that takes solutions from nature in this way is called biomimicry.

## Why it works

Experts in one field tend to know the same ideas. Another field may have solved a structurally similar problem long ago. The mind finds it easy to apply a known solution to a new setting when the structure matches, but it needs prompting to look beyond its own area.

## Types of analogy

| Type | How it works | Prompt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct | Compare with something in another field or nature | How does nature or another industry solve this? |
| Personal | Imagine being the thing or the user | What does it feel like to be this system? |
| Symbolic | Use a short, evocative phrase that captures the essence | What phrase captures the problem in a paradox? |
| Fantasy | Imagine the ideal, even impossible, solution | If magic were allowed, what would happen? |

## When to use

- Standard solutions in your field have not worked
- You want to approach a problem from an unfamiliar angle
- You are designing something new and have no model
- A group's thinking is too uniform

## When not to use

- The problem is purely numeric or logical
- A proven standard solution exists and fits
- You cannot find a structural link; do not force a weak analogy

## How to apply

1. **State the problem** and the function you need, such as "keep something cool", "guide people", or "spread information".
2. **Abstract it.** Remove field-specific words and restate the problem in general terms: "move many items through a bottleneck", not "speed up the airport check-in".
3. **Look for matches.** Ask where else this general problem exists: nature, other industries, history, games, everyday life. Make a list.
4. **Study the best matches.** For each, describe how the problem is solved there and why it works.
5. **Map back.** Translate each solution to your own context. What corresponds to what?
6. **Check the fit.** Where does the analogy break down? What must change?
7. **Choose and test.** Pick the most promising idea and test a small version.

## Example: queues at a clinic reception

**Problem:** Patients queue at a clinic reception, and waiting is stressful.

**Abstracted:** Many people need a small piece of service from one point, and urgency varies.

| Analogy | How it is solved there | Idea for the clinic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deli counter | Take a numbered ticket and wait nearby | Numbered tickets and a seating area, with no standing line |
| Air traffic control | Planes are sequenced by priority and slot | Time-slot booking with urgent cases flagged |
| Restaurant pager | Guests are alerted when the table is ready | Text message when it is the patient's turn, so they can wait outside |
| Airline online check-in | Do the form before arrival | Pre-fill forms online before the visit |
| Triage in emergency care | Sort by urgency on arrival | A short sorting step at the door |

**Result:** The clinic adopts pre-visit forms, numbered tickets and text alerts. The line at the desk becomes much shorter, and the waiting time feels less stressful.

## Pitfalls

- **Surface analogies.** Pick comparisons with a similar structure, not just similar words.
- **Forcing the fit.** If it does not map, try another.
- **Stopping at the analogy.** Translate it into a concrete change.
- **Staying in your own field.** Deliberately look far away.
- **Skipping the abstraction step.** Without it, you only find close neighbours.
- **Ignoring where the analogy breaks.** A solution that works in nature or in another industry may rely on conditions you lack.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Function of the problem stated
- [ ] Problem restated in general terms
- [ ] Several analogies from different fields
- [ ] Solution understood in its original setting
- [ ] Mapped back, with differences noted
- [ ] Small test planned
