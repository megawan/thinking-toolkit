# Regret Minimization

> Imagine yourself at 80 looking back, and choose the path you would regret least.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Decision making |
| **Time needed** | 15-30 minutes of quiet reflection |
| **Best for** | Big personal or career choices where the numbers do not settle it |

## What it is

The regret minimization framework asks you to project yourself into the future, for example to age 80, and to look back at the choice. You compare which option you would regret more: trying and failing, or never trying.

Jeff Bezos described using this approach in 1994 when he decided to leave a secure job in finance to start an online bookstore, which became Amazon. He said that he did not expect to regret trying, even if it failed, but would likely regret never having tried.

## Why it works

Day-to-day pressures, such as fear of embarrassment, short-term cost, and what others will think, shrink when seen from a long distance. People tend to regret what they did not do more than what they did, because action can be explained and learned from, while inaction leaves an unanswered "what if". Zooming out to the end of a life or career moves attention from the fear of the moment to what matters over time.

## When to use

- A major life or career choice, such as a job change, a move, a venture, or a relationship
- The pros and cons are balanced, and analysis does not break the tie
- You feel pulled to play safe, and want to check whether that is what you value
- A choice you will only get to make once, or one that is hard to reverse

## When not to use

- The decision is routine or easily reversed
- It can be settled with data, for example by [Cost-Benefit Analysis](cost-benefit-analysis.md)
- Impulsive risks that could cause serious harm; regret is about values, not a licence for recklessness

## How to apply

1. **State the choice** and the realistic options, including staying as you are.
2. **Pick a distant point,** such as age 80 or the end of your career, and imagine you are there.
3. **Look back at each option.** Write down what you would feel and say about each choice.
4. **Ask two questions for each option:**
   - If I did this and it failed, would I regret it?
   - If I did not do this, would I wonder what might have happened?
5. **Separate the types of regret.** Regret of action (I tried and it went wrong) and regret of inaction (I never tried) are not the same. Note which is stronger for each option.
6. **Check your values.** Which option fits the person you want to have been?
7. **Test with the downside.** Can you live with the worst realistic outcome of the option you lean towards? If not, look for a way to limit the risk.
8. **Decide, and plan the first step.**

## Prompt questions

- At 80, which of these choices would I tell a young person about with pride?
- What would I wish I had at least tried?
- What am I afraid of, and will it matter in thirty years?
- Am I choosing this because it is right, or because it is comfortable?
- What would I advise a close friend in my position?

## Example: leaving a stable job to start a business

**Choice:** Stay in a stable job at an engineering firm, or leave to start a small design studio.

| Option | Regret if it goes wrong | Regret of never trying |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stay | Low risk, but a pattern of waiting for the perfect time | High: "I always wanted to try this" |
| Leave | Money stress, possible return to employment after a year | Low: tried and learned |

**Worst case:** The studio fails and the person returns to a similar job after a year with savings used up. They judge that survivable.

**Decision:** Leave, but limit the risk by saving six months of living costs first and signing two clients before resigning.

## Pitfalls

- **Using it to justify impulse.** Pair it with a check on the downside.
- **Ignoring other people.** Responsibilities to family and dependants are part of what you would regret.
- **Imagining only the good outcomes.** Picture failure honestly.
- **Treating it as the only method.** Use it with numbers and advice, not instead of them.
- **Too distant to be useful.** For some choices, 10 years is more realistic than 80.
- **Confusing regret with fear.** Fear of change is not the same as a lasting wish.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Options named, including staying put
- [ ] Looked back from a far point in time
- [ ] Regret of action and inaction compared
- [ ] Worst realistic case faced
- [ ] Risk limited where possible
- [ ] First step planned
