# Expected Value and Opportunity Cost

> Weigh each outcome by its chance, and count what you give up by not choosing the next-best option.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Decision making |
| **Time needed** | 20-45 minutes |
| **Best for** | Choices under uncertainty, and any choice where time or money has alternative uses |

## What it is

Two ideas that work well together.

**Expected value (EV)** is the average result of a choice if you could repeat it many times. For each possible outcome, multiply its value by its probability, then add them up. The concept dates to the correspondence between Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat in 1654 about games of chance.

**Opportunity cost** is the value of the best alternative you give up when you choose something. It is not always a cash cost, and it is often the largest cost in a decision. The term is credited to the Austrian economist Friedrich von Wieser.

## Why it works

People overweight vivid outcomes and ignore probabilities. Expected value forces you to put numbers on both the size and the likelihood of results. Opportunity cost reminds you that every choice excludes others, so the question is not "is this good?" but "is this better than the next-best use of the same time and money?"

## The formulas

| Concept | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Expected value | EV = sum of (probability x value) for each outcome, with probabilities adding up to 100% |
| Net gain over the alternative | EV of the option - value of the best alternative (the opportunity cost) |

## When to use

- Outcomes are uncertain and you can estimate chances and values
- You can repeat similar bets, so averages matter
- Time or money has competing uses
- You want to compare a risky option with a safe one

## When not to use

- A single outcome could be ruinous. A low-probability disaster can matter more than the average suggests.
- You cannot estimate probabilities even roughly
- Non-monetary values dominate

## How to apply

1. **Define the options,** including the next-best alternative and doing nothing.
2. **List the possible outcomes** of each option. Include the bad ones.
3. **Estimate the probability of each outcome.** Probabilities for one option must sum to 100%. Use data, base rates or honest judgement, and write down the range.
4. **Estimate the value of each outcome** in the same unit, such as money or hours. Include costs.
5. **Calculate EV** for each option.
6. **Identify the opportunity cost** of each option: the value of the best alternative forgone.
7. **Compare net gain** over the alternative.
8. **Check the risk.** Look at the worst case and the spread. Can you survive it?
9. **Test sensitivity.** Change the key probabilities and see if the choice flips.
10. **Decide,** and record your estimates so you can learn from the results.

## Example: launching a product vs. taking a consulting contract

**Option A:** Spend three months launching a product.

| Outcome | Probability | Profit | Probability x profit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Big success | 30% | $200,000 | $60,000 |
| Modest success | 50% | $20,000 | $10,000 |
| Failure | 20% | -$60,000 | -$12,000 |
| **EV of A** | 100% | | **$58,000** |

**Option B:** Take a three-month consulting contract for a certain $45,000. This is the best alternative, so its value is the opportunity cost of A.

| Measure | Result |
| --- | --- |
| EV of A | $58,000 |
| Opportunity cost of A (value of B) | $45,000 |
| Net gain of A over B | $13,000 |

**Reading it:** A has the higher average, but in one case in five the owner loses $60,000. If that loss would be a serious problem, B, or a smaller version of A, may be wiser.

**Sensitivity check:** With 20% big success, 55% modest and 25% failure, EV = 40,000 + 11,000 - 15,000 = $36,000, which is below B's $45,000. The choice depends on the success estimate, so the owner tests demand before committing.

## Everyday opportunity cost

| Choice | Opportunity cost |
| --- | --- |
| Spending a Saturday on a low-paid task | What else that day could earn or give: rest, income, time with family |
| Keeping money in a low-interest account | The return it could earn elsewhere, weighed against the risk |
| Attending a long meeting | The work not done in those hours |
| Studying a course | Other courses, or the income from work during that time |

## Pitfalls

- **Treating estimates as facts.** Show a range, and test sensitivity.
- **Ignoring ruin.** A positive EV does not make a bet safe if one outcome is unsurvivable.
- **Forgetting the opportunity cost.** Comparing only with "nothing" overstates gains.
- **Overconfidence in probabilities.** People are often too sure of success.
- **Counting sunk costs.** Money already spent is not part of the decision.
- **Using money only.** Add time, stress and strategic value where they matter.
- **Mixing up opportunity cost with price.** A free option can have a high opportunity cost.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Options and next-best alternative named
- [ ] Outcomes listed, including bad ones
- [ ] Probabilities sum to 100% per option
- [ ] Values in one unit
- [ ] EV and opportunity cost computed
- [ ] Worst case survivable
- [ ] Sensitivity tested
