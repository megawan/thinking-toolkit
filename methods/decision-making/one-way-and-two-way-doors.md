# One-way and Two-way Doors

> Sort decisions by how hard they are to undo, then decide small ones fast and big ones carefully.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Decision making |
| **Time needed** | 2-5 minutes per decision |
| **Best for** | Teams and individuals who spend too long on easy decisions, or too little on hard ones |

## What it is

This model sorts decisions into two types based on reversibility.

| Type | Also called | What it means | How to treat it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One-way door | Type 1 | Hard or impossible to reverse; walking through means living with it | Decide slowly, with analysis and the right people |
| Two-way door | Type 2 | Easy to reverse; you can walk back through | Decide quickly, with a small group or one person |

Jeff Bezos described the idea in Amazon's shareholder letter for 2015, published in 2016. He observed that most decisions are two-way doors, and that treating them all like one-way doors makes organisations slow and risk-averse.

## Why it works

The cost of a wrong decision depends mostly on how hard it is to undo. For a reversible choice, the main cost of deciding slowly is lost time, and the cost of a mistake is small. For an irreversible one, a mistake is costly and extra care is worthwhile. Matching effort to reversibility saves time on the many and protects the few that matter.

## When to use

- A team is stuck in long meetings over minor decisions
- You are about to commit to something large and want a check
- You need to delegate decisions and want a rule for who decides what
- You want to turn a one-way decision into a safer, reversible one

## When not to use

- You already know a decision is irreversible; go straight to a deeper method
- The sorting itself becomes a debate; time-box it

## How to apply

1. **Name the decision** in a sentence.
2. **Ask: can we undo this?** If we are wrong, what would it take to reverse, in time, money and reputation?
3. **Classify it.** Easy to reverse is two-way; hard or impossible is one-way. Many decisions are in between.
4. **Match the process.**
   - Two-way: decide now with the person closest to the problem. Set a review date.
   - One-way: gather data, consult, consider alternatives, and use a method such as [Decision Matrix](decision-matrix.md) or [Pre-mortem](../strategy-and-risk/pre-mortem.md).
5. **Try to turn one-way into two-way.** Pilot it, test it, rent before buying, or use a short contract.
6. **Record the decision** and the signal that would make you reverse it.
7. **Commit.** Even where you disagreed, support the decision once it is made, and review at the date.

## Questions to ask

- What is the worst case if we are wrong?
- How long, and how much, would it take to undo?
- Who would be affected, and can they recover?
- Can we test a smaller version first?
- What would tell us early that we were wrong?

## Examples

| Decision | Type | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trying a new meeting format for a month | Two-way | One person decides; review in four weeks |
| Changing the colour of a button | Two-way | Designer decides; measure results |
| Signing a five-year office lease | One-way (mostly) | Compare options, take legal advice, run a pre-mortem |
| Choosing the core technology for a new product | One-way (costly to reverse) | Prototype the riskiest parts, then decide with the team |
| Hiring a senior leader | One-way (costly to reverse) | Structured interviews, references, a trial project |
| Launching a pilot in one city | Two-way | Decide fast; scale or stop after results |

## Example: turning a one-way door into a two-way door

**Decision:** Buy a $30,000 machine for a new product line.

- **First view:** It is a one-way door; the money is spent, and resale value is low.
- **Making it safer:** Rent the machine for three months, or buy a used model, or outsource a first batch to test demand.
- **Result:** The team pays a small amount to learn whether the product sells before committing.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating everything as one-way.** It causes slowness and fear.
- **Treating one-way decisions as two-way.** Speed is wrong when mistakes are permanent.
- **Ignoring the middle.** Some decisions are costly to reverse but not impossible; give them a medium level of care.
- **Never reviewing.** A two-way door only helps if you check and reverse when needed.
- **Assuming a door is two-way because the first step is.** A small step can lead to a commitment, such as a free trial that auto-renews.
- **Letting the sorting take longer than the decision.** Time-box it.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Decision named
- [ ] Cost and time to reverse estimated
- [ ] Classified as one-way, two-way or in between
- [ ] Process matched to the type
- [ ] Ways to make it reversible considered
- [ ] Review date and warning signal set
