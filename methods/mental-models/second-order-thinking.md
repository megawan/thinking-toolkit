# Second-order Thinking

> Ask "and then what?" so you see the consequences of the consequences, not only the first effect.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Category** | Mental models |
| **Time needed** | 15-30 minutes |
| **Best for** | Policies, incentives, product changes and decisions where the first result looks good but later effects may not |

## What it is

First-order thinking looks at the immediate result of an action. Second-order thinking looks at what that result leads to, and third-order thinking goes one step further. The aim is to follow the chain of effects far enough to see where the decision actually ends up.

The idea has a long history. The sociologist Robert K. Merton wrote about the "unanticipated consequences" of purposeful action in 1936. The ecologist Garrett Hardin summarised the habit as asking "and then what?". In investing, Howard Marks calls the same skill "second-level thinking".

## Why it works

Most people stop at the first effect, because it is visible and quick. But systems respond: people adapt, competitors react, and incentives change behaviour. Many bad decisions have a good first-order effect and a bad second-order effect. Looking ahead one or two more steps catches these before you commit.

## When to use

- Setting a rule, a target, a price or an incentive
- Making a change that many people will react to
- A decision looks too good: easy benefit, no visible cost
- Evaluating policies or product changes

## When not to use

- The decision is small and reversible; a quick test is cheaper
- You cannot name the actors or the mechanism; gather facts first
- You are tempted to follow the chain forever; stop at the point where effects become too uncertain

## How to apply

1. **State the decision** and its intended effect.
2. **List the first-order effects:** the direct, immediate results.
3. **Ask "and then what?"** for each one. Who reacts? What do they do?
4. **List the second-order effects.** Include effects on different groups: customers, staff, competitors, regulators, your future self.
5. **Repeat once more** for the important ones to find third-order effects.
6. **Sort by timing and size.** Which effects come soon and which later? Which are large?
7. **Look for loops.** Does an effect feed back into the cause, making it stronger or weaker?
8. **Adjust the decision** to keep the good effects and reduce the bad ones, or add a safeguard.
9. **Set indicators** you will watch to see if the effects appear.

## Prompt questions

- And then what happens?
- Who will respond to this, and how?
- What behaviour does this reward?
- What happens if everyone does it?
- What happens in a year? In five?
- Who gains and who loses at each step?

## Example 1: a bonus for closing support tickets

**Decision:** Pay support staff a bonus for each ticket closed.

| Order | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| First | Staff close tickets faster; the closed count rises |
| Second | Staff close tickets before the problem is solved; customers reopen them or complain; easy tickets are picked and hard ones left |
| Third | Customer satisfaction falls; hard issues pile up; the best agents leave because the target rewards speed over skill |

**Adjustment:** Reward first-contact resolution and customer satisfaction, and count reopened tickets against the bonus.

## Example 2: banning cars from a city centre

**Decision:** Close the centre to cars.

| Order | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| First | Less traffic and noise; more space for pedestrians |
| Second | Footfall may rise; some shops lose delivery access; traffic moves to neighbouring streets |
| Third | Rents rise in the centre; some businesses move out; the pressure for public transport grows |

**Adjustment:** Plan delivery windows, improve public transport before the ban, and monitor traffic on nearby streets.

## Pitfalls

- **Stopping at the first order.** The most common error.
- **Imagining endless chains.** Effects become guesses after two or three steps.
- **Seeing only bad effects.** Look for good second-order effects as well.
- **Ignoring other people's reactions.** Most second-order effects come from them.
- **Paralysis.** The goal is a better decision, not a perfect forecast.
- **No follow-up.** Watch whether the predicted effects appear.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Decision and intended effect stated
- [ ] First-order effects listed
- [ ] "And then what?" asked for each
- [ ] Reactions of other groups considered
- [ ] Feedback loops noted
- [ ] Decision adjusted or safeguard added
- [ ] Indicators chosen
